\section{Tariffs and Exchange-Rate Adjustment: A General Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{The bilateral case}
\label{sec:bilateral}

Consider two countries, $A$ and $B$. Let $e_{AB}$ denote units of $A$'s currency per unit of $B$'s, so that a rise is a depreciation of $A$, and write $\nu\equiv\log e_{AB}$. Country $A$ levies an ad valorem tariff $\tau$ on imports from $B$. Equilibrium with balanced trade is $\TB_A(\nu,\tau)=0$. Linearizing,
\begin{equation}
d\,\TB_A=\TB_{A\nu}\,d\nu+\TB_{A\tau}\,d\tau=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
d\nu^*=-\frac{\TB_{A\tau}}{\TB_{A\nu}}\,d\tau,
\label{eq:bilat}
\end{equation}
where $\TB_{A\nu}\equiv\partial\TB_A/\partial\nu$ and $\TB_{A\tau}\equiv\partial\TB_A/\partial\tau$ at fixed $\nu$. A tariff raises the domestic price of imports and, at unchanged exchange rates, improves $A$'s trade balance ($\TB_{A\tau}>0$). The response is then an appreciation if and only if $\TB_{A\nu}>0$, that is, if and only if a depreciation improves the trade balance. At a balanced baseline $\TB_{A\nu}=X(\varepsilon_X+\varepsilon_M-1)$, where $X$ is the value of exports and $\varepsilon_X,\varepsilon_M$ are the export and import demand elasticities, so this is the Marshall--Lerner condition $\varepsilon_X+\varepsilon_M>1$. \Cref{fig:bilateral} illustrates.

\begin{remark}[Robustness of the two-country result]
\label{rem:robustness}
The sign $d\nu^*/d\tau<0$ requires only that (i) import expenditure falls with its consumer price and the tariff does not reduce export revenue by more at fixed $\nu$, and (ii) Marshall--Lerner holds. These conditions are satisfied under CES, nested-CES and quasi-linear preferences, under diminishing returns or sector-specific factors with upward-sloping export supply, and under any standard treatment of tariff revenue (Online \cref{app:online-twocountry}). The correct reading is not that appreciation holds in every model, but that \textbf{the two-country result holds wherever Marshall--Lerner holds}. The rest of the paper shows that with three or more countries this is no longer enough.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The disturbance--adjustment representation}
\label{sec:representation}

Now consider $N$ countries. Take $A$'s currency as numeraire. The $N-1$ bilateral rates $e_{Aj}$, $j\neq A$, span all exchange-rate changes, and Walras' law leaves $N-1$ independent trade-balance conditions:
\begin{equation*}
\TB_i\big(\{e_{Aj}\}_{j\neq A},\{\tau_{jk}\}_{j\neq k}\big)=0,\qquad i\neq A.
\end{equation*}
Stack $\nu=(\nu_j)_{j\neq A}$ with $\nu_j=\log e_{Aj}$, and the bilateral tariff changes as $d\tau=(d\tau_{jk})$. Linearizing,
\begin{equation}
J\,d\nu+G\,d\tau=0,\qquad J_{il}\equiv\frac{\partial\TB_i}{\partial\nu_l},\qquad G_{i,(jk)}\equiv\frac{\partial\TB_i}{\partial\tau_{jk}}\bigg|_{\nu}.
\label{eq:JG}
\end{equation}
Write a tariff change as a direction $a$ ($\|a\|=1$) times a scale $ds$, and let $F\equiv Ga$. Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\frac{d\nu}{ds}=(-J)^{-1}F.\;}
\label{eq:response}
\end{equation}
This separates two conceptually distinct objects. $F$ is the \textbf{tariff incidence}: the vector of trade-balance disturbances at unchanged exchange rates, including expenditure switching, tariff revenue and income effects. $(-J)^{-1}$ is the \textbf{exchange-rate adjustment}: the movements needed to offset any given vector of disturbances. In the bilateral case both are scalars, and \cref{eq:response} reduces to \cref{eq:bilat}. With $N\geq3$, $F$ can have entries of different signs, because a tariff on one partner may \emph{improve} another partner's trade balance.

\subsection{The multilateral Marshall--Lerner condition}
\label{sec:mml}

\begin{definition}[Multilateral Marshall--Lerner condition, MML]
\label{def:mml}
The adjustment mechanism satisfies MML if $J_{il}\geq0$ for $l\neq i$ and $(-J)^{-1}$ exists with $(-J)^{-1}\geq0$ elementwise. Equivalently, $-J$ is a nonsingular M-matrix.
\end{definition}

Under MML, writing $\vartheta_{ij}\equiv[(-J)^{-1}]_{ij}\geq0$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\nu_i}{ds}=\sum_j\vartheta_{ij}F_j.
\label{eq:signrule}
\end{equation}
A positive disturbance in any country contributes weakly positively to every bilateral response. So \textbf{a uniformly signed $F$ cannot generate responses of the opposite sign}, which is the multilateral version of the two-country logic. When $F$ has mixed signs, the response of any bilateral rate depends on the whole vector of disturbances, not on its own entry.

A more primitive sufficient condition uses the full $N\times N$ Jacobian $\tilde J_{il}=\partial\TB_i/\partial\nu_l$ in terms of currency values, where now $\nu_l$ is the log value of currency $l$ in a common unit of account. Homogeneity of degree one gives $\sum_l\tilde J_{il}=\TB_i=0$ at a balanced baseline, and Walras' law gives $\sum_i\tilde J_{il}=0$.

\begin{assumption}[Gross substitutability of trade balances]
\label{ass:gs}
$\tilde J_{il}\geq0$ for all $l\neq i$: an appreciation of currency $l$ weakly improves every other country's trade balance.
\end{assumption}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:gs}
If each trade balance is homogeneous of degree one in currency values, \cref{ass:gs} holds, $J$ is irreducible, and $\tilde J_{iA}>0$ for some $i$, then MML holds.
\end{lemma}

This is the trade-balance analogue of the gross-substitutes stability results of \citet{hicks1939}, \citet{mosak1944}, \citet{metzler1945} and \citet{arrow1959}. We use it as the multilateral counterpart of the Marshall--Lerner condition. The point to stress is that \textbf{MML does not pin down the sign of any individual exchange-rate response.} Every bilateral Marshall--Lerner condition can hold and the multilateral response can still go either way. What decides it is the structure of $F$.

\subsection{Symmetric partners}
\label{sec:decomposition}

The representation \cref{eq:response} holds at any balanced baseline. When the tariffing country's partners are symmetric, it can be solved in closed form in terms of four eigenvalues.

Restrict attention to tariff changes imposed by $A$, $t=(t_j)_{j\neq A}$ with $t_j\equiv d\tau_{Aj}$, and let $G$ now denote the $(N-1)\times(N-1)$ restriction of the tariff-incidence matrix. Suppose $A$'s partners are \textbf{symmetric}: $PJP'=J$ and $PGP'=G$ for every permutation $P$ of the partners, as at free trade or under a common external tariff. Decompose
\begin{equation}
t=\bar t\,\bone+\tilde t,\qquad \bar t=\tfrac{1}{N-1}\bone't,\qquad \bone'\tilde t=0.
\label{eq:tsplit}
\end{equation}
We call $\bar t$ \textbf{average protection} and $\tilde t$ \textbf{relative treatment}. Any permutation-invariant matrix has the form $aI+b\bone\bone'$, so $\bone$ and every zero-sum vector are eigenvectors of both $G$ and $-J$:
\begin{equation*}
G\bone=g_S\bone,\quad Gv=g_Dv;\qquad (-J)\bone=j_S\bone,\quad (-J)v=j_Dv\qquad(\bone'v=0).
\end{equation*}

\begin{proposition}[Average protection and relative treatment]
\label{prop:decomposition}
Let partners be symmetric, $N\geq3$, and $j_S,j_D\neq0$. For any tariff vector $t$,
\begin{equation}
d\nu=\lambda_A\,\bar t\,\bone+\lambda_R\,\tilde t,\qquad \lambda_A=g_S/j_S,\quad \lambda_R=g_D/j_D.
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
Hence, with $d\nu^E_A\equiv\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq A}d\nu_j$ (the equally weighted effective rate),
\begin{equation}
d\nu^E_A=\lambda_A\bar t,\qquad d\nu_j-d\nu^E_A=\lambda_R\tilde t_j,\qquad d\log e_{jk}=\lambda_R(t_k-t_j).
\label{eq:decompparts}
\end{equation}
Moreover, MML is equivalent to $j_S>0$ and $j_D\geq j_S$.
\end{proposition}

\textbf{Average protection alone moves the effective rate; relative treatment alone moves bilateral rates around it and the cross rates among partners.} The geometry is simple. The response vector lives in the plane spanned by the common direction $\bone$ and the zero-sum directions, and each component is scaled by its own coefficient (\cref{fig:geometry}).
