\section{Retaliation and Multilateral Tariff Configurations}
\label{sec:tradewars}

How does retaliation change the balance between average protection and relative treatment? Under a unilateral tariff, average protection pushes the tariffing country's currency toward appreciation, while favorable relative treatment pushes untariffed bystanders in the opposite direction. Retaliation strengthens this second force. As a result, the bystander-depreciation threshold falls from $\rho>N\rho_D$ under a unilateral tariff to $\rho>\rho_D$.

\subsection{Retaliation}

\begin{proposition}[Retaliation and trade wars]
\label{prop:retaliation}
At the symmetric benchmark with $N\geq3$ under MML, to first order:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item Under bilateral retaliation ($A$ tariffs $B$ and $B$ tariffs $A$ by $d\tau$), $d\nu_B=0$ and
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\nu_c}{d\tau}=\frac{\rho-\rho_D}{(N-1)M_N},\qquad
\frac{d\nu^E_A}{d\tau}=\frac{N-2}{N-1}\cdot\frac{\rho-\rho_D}{(N-1)M_N}.
\end{equation}
Both belligerents depreciate against every bystander, and in effective terms, if and only if $\rho>\rho_D$. Cross rates among bystanders are unchanged.
\item Under a multilateral trade war ($A$ tariffs every partner, and every partner retaliates against $A$), $d\nu_j=d\nu^E_A=\frac{(N-2)(\rho-\rho_D)}{(N-1)M_N}\,d\tau$ for all $j$, so $A$ depreciates against every partner if and only if $\rho>\rho_D$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Under bilateral retaliation, the average-protection effects on the belligerents' bilateral rate cancel, leaving that rate unchanged. Against a bystander, however, the relative-treatment effects reinforce each other. The bystander threshold therefore falls from $N\rho_D$ under a unilateral tariff to $\rho_D$. When $\rho>\rho_D$, both belligerents depreciate against the bystanders and in effective terms.

A multilateral trade war produces the same threshold but shifts the location of adjustment. Under bilateral retaliation, the belligerents' bilateral rate is unchanged and adjustment occurs against the bystanders. In a multilateral trade war there are no untreated bystanders, so all of the tariffing country's bilateral rates move together. In both cases, retaliation increases the importance of relative treatment relative to average protection. \Cref{fig:configurations} compares these responses directly. Under a unilateral tariff, the bystander response changes sign at $3\rho_D$; under bilateral retaliation, the threshold falls to $\rho_D$.

\subsection{Connection to Preferential Trade}

The bystander response is also a third-country terms-of-trade effect of discriminatory protection, connecting the analysis to the preferential-trade literature. In the canonical competing-exporter model, a tariff on one exporter improves the terms of trade of an untariffed exporter because demand is diverted toward it. In the present model, the tariffing country also produces a competing tradable, so diverted expenditure can instead shift toward domestic production.

The bystander therefore gains only when cross-origin substitution is sufficiently strong relative to domestic diversion. The formal comparison with the competing-exporter model, extensions to a positive common external tariff, and results for discrete preferential cuts are reported in the online supplement.
