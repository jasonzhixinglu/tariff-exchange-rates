\section{Retaliation and Relative Treatment}
\label{sec:tradewars}

How does retaliation change the balance between average protection and relative treatment? Under a unilateral tariff, average protection pushes $A$'s currency toward appreciation, while the favorable relative treatment of bystanders pushes in the opposite direction. Retaliation adds a second relative-treatment effect in the bystanders' favor without adding an equivalent average-protection effect.

Because the analysis is local, the exchange-rate responses to simultaneous tariff changes can be obtained by superposition. The main result is that retaliation lowers the bystander-depreciation threshold from $\rho>N\rho_D$ under a unilateral tariff to $\rho>\rho_D$.

\subsection{Bilateral retaliation and multilateral trade wars}
\label{sec:retaliation}

\begin{proposition}[Retaliation and trade wars]
\label{prop:retaliation}
At the symmetric benchmark with $N\geq3$ under MML, to first order:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item Under bilateral retaliation ($A$ tariffs $B$ and $B$ tariffs $A$ by $d\tau$), $d\nu_B=0$ and
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\nu_c}{d\tau}=\frac{\rho-\rho_D}{(N-1)M_N},\qquad
\frac{d\nu^E_A}{d\tau}=\frac{N-2}{N-1}\cdot\frac{\rho-\rho_D}{(N-1)M_N}.
\label{eq:retaliation}
\end{equation}
Both belligerents depreciate against every bystander, and in effective terms, if and only if $\rho>\rho_D$. Cross rates among bystanders are unchanged.
\item Under a multilateral trade war ($A$ tariffs every partner, and every partner retaliates against $A$), $d\nu_j=d\nu^E_A=\frac{(N-2)(\rho-\rho_D)}{(N-1)M_N}\,d\tau$ for all $j$, so $A$ depreciates against every partner if and only if $\rho>\rho_D$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Under bilateral retaliation, the two average-protection effects on the belligerents' bilateral rate cancel, leaving that rate unchanged. Against a bystander, however, the relative-treatment effects reinforce each other. The bystander threshold therefore falls from $N\rho_D$ under a unilateral tariff to $\rho_D$, the point at which the bystander's trade balance improves at unchanged exchange rates. When $\rho>\rho_D$, both belligerents depreciate against the bystanders and in effective terms. At the US-like calibration, $\rho_D=1.32$, below the illustrative range $\rho\in[2,4]$ shown in \cref{fig:configurations}.

A multilateral trade war produces the same threshold but shifts the location of adjustment. Under bilateral retaliation, the belligerents' bilateral rate is unchanged and adjustment occurs against the bystanders. In a multilateral trade war there are no untreated bystanders, so all of $A$'s bilateral rates move together. In both cases, retaliation increases the importance of relative treatment relative to average protection.

\subsection{Connection to preferential trade}
\label{sec:pta}

The bystander response is also a third-country terms-of-trade effect of discriminatory protection, connecting the analysis to the preferential-trade literature. In the canonical competing-exporter model, a tariff on one exporter improves the terms of trade of an untariffed exporter because demand is diverted toward it. In the present model, the tariffing country also produces a competing tradable, so diverted expenditure can instead shift toward domestic production.

The bystander therefore gains only when cross-origin substitution is sufficiently strong relative to domestic diversion. The formal comparison with the competing-exporter model, the extension to a positive common external tariff, and the results for discrete preferential cuts are reported in \cref{app:asym-tariff}.
