\section{The Nested-CES Model}
\label{sec:nestedces}

This section specializes the decomposition of \cref{sec:decomposition} into average protection and relative treatment to a nested-CES trade model. In the model, $\eta$ governs substitution between the domestic tradable and the import composite, and $\rho$ governs substitution across foreign origins. We work locally, to first order around a symmetric, balanced, free-trade benchmark. The central result is that a unilateral tariff always appreciates the tariffing country's NEER and its currency against the target, but depreciates it against untariffed bystanders only when $\rho>N\rho_D$, where $\rho_D$ is a domestic diversion margin defined below. With weak home bias, $\alpha_D\geq1/N$, a specification with a single elasticity, $\rho=\eta$, cannot produce this reversal.

\subsection{Production, preferences and equilibrium}
\label{sec:model}

There are $N\geq2$ countries, $i\in\{1,\dots,N\}$. Country $A$ is the tariffing country, $B$ is the targeted partner, and lowercase $c$ denotes an individual untariffed bystander; uppercase $C$ is reserved for the composite bystander of \cref{sec:composite}. Each country is endowed with $L_i$ units of labor, mobile across sectors, and produces a country-specific tradable and a non-tradable with labor only, $Y_{Ti}=A_{Ti}L_{Ti}$ and $Y_{Ni}=A_{Ni}L_{Ni}$, with $L_{Ti}+L_{Ni}=L_i$. Normalizing each tradable's producer price to one in its own currency gives $w_i=A_{Ti}$ and $P_{Ni}=A_{Ti}/A_{Ni}$. Relative international producer prices therefore move only through exchange rates, and the bilateral exchange rate equals the bilateral terms of trade and the relative wage in a common currency.

Preferences are
\begin{equation}
C_i=C_{Ti}^{\alpha_T}C_{Ni}^{1-\alpha_T},\quad
C_{Ti}=\Big[\alpha_D^{1/\eta}C_{Tii}^{\frac{\eta-1}{\eta}}+(1-\alpha_D)^{1/\eta}M_i^{\frac{\eta-1}{\eta}}\Big]^{\frac{\eta}{\eta-1}},\quad
M_i=\Big[\textstyle\sum_{j\neq i}\beta_{ij}^{1/\rho}C_{Tji}^{\frac{\rho-1}{\rho}}\Big]^{\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}},
\label{eq:prefs}
\end{equation}
with $0<\alpha_D<1$, $0<\alpha_T\leq1$, $\eta,\rho>0$ (read as Cobb--Douglas at unit elasticity), $\beta_{ij}>0$ and $\sum_{j\neq i}\beta_{ij}=1$. Country $i$ levies an ad valorem tariff $\tau_{ij}$ on imports from $j$, so the consumer price is $p_{ij}=(1+\tau_{ij})e_{ij}$, and the import and tradable price indices are $P_{Mi}=[\sum_{j\neq i}\beta_{ij}p_{ij}^{1-\rho}]^{1/(1-\rho)}$ and $P_{Ti}=[\alpha_D+(1-\alpha_D)P_{Mi}^{1-\eta}]^{1/(1-\eta)}$. Income is
\begin{equation}
I_i=\frac{w_iL_i}{1-\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\tau_{ij}}{1+\tau_{ij}}s_{ij}},
\label{eq:income}
\end{equation}
where $s_{ij}$ is the tariff-inclusive expenditure share of income on $j$'s good. Tariff revenue is rebated lump-sum and is a domestic transfer, so the external-balance conditions use flows at border prices:
\begin{equation}
\TB_i=\sum_{k\neq i}f_{ki}-\sum_{j\neq i}f_{ij},\qquad f_{ij}=\frac{s_{ij}\tilde I_i}{1+\tau_{ij}},
\label{eq:tb}
\end{equation}
with $\tilde I_i=e_{Ai}I_i$ income in $A$'s currency. Under the normalization $v_A=1$ of \cref{sec:bilateral}, values in $A$'s currency equal values in the common unit of account, so \cref{eq:tb} is the trade-balance measure of \cref{sec:framework}. With $A$'s currency as numeraire, the $N-1$ independent conditions $\TB_i=0$, $i\neq A$, determine the $N-1$ bilateral rates; wages, prices and expenditure shares follow from the technologies and the demand system \cref{eq:prefs}.

We write $\nu_j=\log e_{Aj}$, with $\nu_A=0$. The NEER is that of \cref{sec:decomposition}, $d\nu^E_A=\sum_{j\neq A}w_{Aj}\,d\nu_j$ with baseline trade weights $w_{Aj}=(f_{Aj}+f_{jA})/\sum_{k\neq A}(f_{Ak}+f_{kA})$. The real exchange rate is $q_{Aj}=e_{Aj}P_j/P_A$, with $P_i$ the consumer price index in own currency. A fall in $\nu_j$ is an appreciation of $A$ against $j$ and a rise a depreciation; a rise in $\log q_{Aj}$ is a real depreciation of $A$ against $j$.

The symmetric benchmark is free trade with $\beta_{ij}=1/(N-1)$, common parameters, and equal endowments and productivities. It retains home bias $\alpha_D$. Bilateral trade weights then equal $1/(N-1)$, and $m\equiv\alpha_T(1-\alpha_D)$ denotes the import share of income.

\subsection{The two-country benchmark}
\label{sec:twocountry}

With $N=2$ there is a single foreign origin, so $\rho$ is inactive. Linearizing gives $d\TB_A=mM_2\,d\nu_B+m\rho_D\,d\tau$, so
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\frac{d\log e_{AB}}{d\tau}\bigg|_{\tau=0}=-\frac{\rho_D}{M_2},\\
\rho_D\equiv\underbrace{\alpha_D\eta}_{\text{domestic substitution}}+\underbrace{(1-\alpha_D)(1-\alpha_T)}_{\text{rebate / non-tradables}},\qquad
M_2\equiv1+2\alpha_D(\eta-1)=\varepsilon_X+\varepsilon_M-1.
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:twocountry}
\end{equation}
We call $\rho_D$ the domestic diversion margin. A tariff shifts expenditure toward the domestic economy in two ways: through substitution toward the domestic tradable, and through the income effect of the rebated revenue, part of which is spent on non-tradables. An exchange-rate movement raises no revenue and so has no rebate term, which is why $\rho_D$ differs from $M_2$. With more than two countries, adjustment also separates into a common movement against all foreign suppliers and relative movements across them.

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$N$}{N}-country case}
\label{sec:theorem}

The following theorem maps the model's primitives into the coefficients of \cref{prop:decomposition}.

\begin{theorem}[Exchange-rate responses in the symmetric nested-CES model]
\label{thm:main}
At the symmetric benchmark define
\begin{equation}
M_N\equiv\frac{1}{N-1}\Big[\,1+\underbrace{N\alpha_D(\eta-1)}_{\text{domestic--import adjustment}}+\underbrace{(N-2)\rho}_{\text{foreign-origin adjustment}}\Big].
\label{eq:MN}
\end{equation}
The eigenvalues of \cref{prop:decomposition} are
\begin{equation}
g_S=-\frac{m\rho_D}{N-1},\qquad g_D=-\frac{m\rho}{N-1},\qquad j_S=\frac{mM_N}{N-1},\qquad j_D=Nj_S.
\label{eq:eigenvalues}
\end{equation}
MML holds if and only if $M_N>0$, and $\eta\geq1$ suffices. When it holds,
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\mathrm{avg}}=-\frac{\rho_D}{M_N},\qquad \lambda_{\mathrm{rel}}=-\frac{\rho}{NM_N},\qquad d\nu_j=\lambda_{\mathrm{avg}}\bar t+\lambda_{\mathrm{rel}}\widetilde t_j.
\label{eq:lambdas}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\noindent The eigenvalues are derived in \cref{lem:jacobian} and \cref{eq:geigen}, and the proof is in \cref{app:proofs}. $M_N$ is affine in $\rho$; we write $M_N\equiv M_N(\rho)$ and suppress the argument except in \cref{sec:real}, where the dependence on $\rho$ matters.

$M_N$ is the sensitivity of $A$'s aggregate trade balance to its effective exchange rate: $d\TB_A=mM_N\,d\nu^E_A$ at the equal weights of the symmetric benchmark. For $N=2$ it reduces to $M_2$. For $N>2$ it includes $\rho$, because foreign buyers can switch between $A$'s goods and those of other foreign suppliers.

The equality $j_D=Nj_S$ means that zero-sum relative exchange-rate movements are $N$ times as effective as common movements in closing trade imbalances, which is why $\lambda_{\mathrm{rel}}$ carries the factor $1/N$. A common movement shifts only partners' trade with $A$, whereas a relative movement also shifts partners' trade with \emph{each other}, and at the symmetric benchmark $A$ accounts for a share $1/N$ of the trade on which relative movements act. Because relative treatment is zero-sum, to first order at the symmetric benchmark it leaves tariff revenue, income and the tradable/non-tradable split unchanged, so its disturbance is governed by $\rho$ alone.

\subsection{A unilateral tariff}
\label{sec:unilateral}

Let $A$ raise its tariff on $B$ alone, $t=d\tau\,e_B$. Its mean component is $\bar t=d\tau/(N-1)$, $B$'s relative component is $\widetilde t_B=\frac{N-2}{N-1}d\tau$, and each bystander's relative component is $\widetilde t_c=-\frac{d\tau}{N-1}$.

\begin{proposition}[Unilateral tariff]
\label{prop:unilateral}
At the symmetric benchmark under MML, to first order,
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\nu^E_A}{d\tau}=-\frac{\rho_D}{(N-1)M_N}<0,\qquad
\frac{d\nu_B}{d\tau}=-\frac{N\rho_D+(N-2)\rho}{N(N-1)M_N}<0,\qquad
\frac{d\nu_c}{d\tau}=\frac{\rho-N\rho_D}{N(N-1)M_N}
\label{eq:unilateral}
\end{equation}
for every untariffed bystander $c$. Hence:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $A$'s NEER appreciates for every $N$ and $\rho$.
\item $A$ appreciates against the target.
\item $A$ depreciates against each untariffed bystander if and only if $\rho>N\rho_D$.
\item The same threshold applies when $A$ tariffs any $k\leq N-2$ partners. More generally, for a tariff vector with $\bar t>0$, $A$ depreciates against a partner $j$ receiving a below-average tariff increase, $t_j<\bar t$, if and only if $t_j/\bar t<1-N\rho_D/\rho$, which requires $\rho>N\rho_D$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Against the target, average protection and relative treatment push in the same direction. Against a bystander they are offsetting pressures: average protection pushes toward an appreciation of $A$, while the bystander's favorable relative treatment pushes toward a depreciation.

\begin{corollary}[No reversal with a single elasticity under weak home bias]
\label{cor:single}
Suppose weak home bias, $\alpha_D\geq1/N$. Then
\begin{equation}
N\rho_D-\eta=\eta(N\alpha_D-1)+N(1-\alpha_D)(1-\alpha_T)\geq0,
\label{eq:single}
\end{equation}
so $\rho>\eta$ is necessary for a bystander depreciation. In any specification with a single elasticity across all tradable varieties ($\rho=\eta=\sigma$), $\rho-N\rho_D=-[\sigma(N\alpha_D-1)+N(1-\alpha_D)(1-\alpha_T)]\leq0$, so wherever MML holds $d\nu_c/d\tau\leq0$: $A$ never depreciates against a bystander. The response is strictly negative except at the knife edge $\alpha_D=1/N$, $\alpha_T=1$, where it is zero for every $\sigma>0$. More generally, at $\alpha_D=1/N$ the response is $-(1-\alpha_T)/(N\sigma)$: if $\alpha_T<1$ it approaches zero from below as $\sigma\to\infty$, and if $\alpha_T=1$ it is identically zero.
\end{corollary}

Under weak home bias, the reversal therefore requires cross-origin substitution to exceed home--import substitution. Evidence in \citet{feenstra2018} and \citet{li2022} supports allowing $\rho$ and $\eta$ to differ. At an illustrative US-like calibration ($\alpha_D=0.8$, $\alpha_T=0.4$, $\eta=1.5$, so $\rho_D=1.32$), the threshold is $N\rho_D=3.96$ for $N=3$ and $6.60$ for $N=5$.

At unchanged exchange rates, the trade balance of an individual bystander improves once $\rho>\rho_D$:
\begin{equation}
F_c=\frac{m(\rho-\rho_D)}{(N-1)^2}.
\label{eq:FC}
\end{equation}
This is not sufficient for $A$ to depreciate against the bystander in equilibrium. Under MML the exchange-rate response depends on the full vector of trade-balance disturbances, including the target's negative disturbance, and reverses only under the stronger condition $\rho>N\rho_D$. Thus, for $\rho_D<\rho<N\rho_D$, the bystander gains at the initial exchange rates even though $A$ ultimately appreciates against it (\cref{fig:geometry-a}). The gap between the two thresholds reflects the greater effectiveness of relative exchange-rate adjustment identified in \cref{thm:main}: if relative and common movements were equally effective, the bystander's equilibrium response would instead change sign when its own disturbance does. \Cref{fig:unilateral} plots the three responses against $\rho$.

\subsection{The role of the bystander bloc}
\label{sec:composite}

The bystander threshold $N\rho_D$ rises with the number of countries. To see why, note that when the bystanders are symmetric and a tariff shock treats them alike, no relative price within the bystander bloc moves, so the bloc can be collapsed into a single region.

\begin{proposition}[Three-region representation]
\label{prop:composite}
At the symmetric benchmark with $N\geq3$, consider a tariff shock that treats the $N-2$ bystanders symmetrically, and suppose $M_N\neq0$ (MML is sufficient). To first order, the response of $\nu_B$, the common response of each bystander, and $A$'s NEER are exactly reproduced by a three-region economy consisting of $A$, $B$ and a composite bystander $C$ with income $N-2$, home share of tradables $h_C=\alpha_D+(1-\alpha_D)\frac{N-3}{N-1}$, and home--import elasticity
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\eta_C=\frac{(N-3)\rho+2\alpha_D\,\eta}{N-3+2\alpha_D}.\;}
\label{eq:etaC}
\end{equation}
The complete mapping of trade weights and tariff shocks is given in \cref{app:proofs}.
\end{proposition}

\noindent The equivalence holds for the linearized systems; it is not an aggregation result for finite tariffs.

Trade among the original bystanders becomes the composite's ``home'' trade, so $h_C$ rises toward one as $N$ grows. In the $N$-country economy that trade is governed by the cross-origin elasticity $\rho$, which is why $\eta_C$ is a weighted average of $\eta$ and $\rho$. As $N$ increases, $\eta_C$ converges to $\rho$; it rises with $N$ if $\rho>\eta$, falls if $\rho<\eta$, and is constant if $\rho=\eta$. Under weak home bias the bystander-reversal region $\rho>N\rho_D$ lies inside $\rho>\eta$.

The higher threshold $N\rho_D$ is therefore not simply a consequence of making the bystander bloc larger. As $N$ rises, a greater share of what the composite treats as ``home'' activity consists of trade among the original bystanders, and that trade adjusts at the cross-origin elasticity $\rho$. This changes the internal adjustment structure of the bloc and gives relative exchange-rate movements their $N$-fold effectiveness at the symmetric benchmark (\cref{thm:main}). \Cref{app:proofs} shows that holding the composite's internal elasticity fixed at $\eta$ eliminates this steep $N$-dependence, confirming that the result reflects the bloc's internal trade structure rather than its size alone.

\subsection{Nominal and real exchange rates}
\label{sec:real}

Let partners impose no tariffs, and define the real effective exchange rate at the symmetric benchmark as $d\log q^E_A\equiv\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq A}d\log q_{Aj}$. To first order,
\begin{equation}
d\log q_{Aj}=\Big(1-\tfrac{mN}{N-1}\Big)d\nu_j-m\bar t,\qquad
d\log q^E_A=\Big[\Big(1-\tfrac{mN}{N-1}\Big)\lambda_{\mathrm{avg}}-m\Big]\bar t.
\label{eq:real}
\end{equation}
The tariff raises $A$'s consumer price index directly, which offsets part of a nominal bystander depreciation. When $m<1/N$ and the real reversal threshold lies in the MML region, it therefore exceeds the nominal threshold; if $\eta\geq1$, MML holds for every $\rho>0$, so this qualification is automatic for a positive threshold.

\begin{proposition}[Real exchange rates]
\label{prop:real}
Consider a unilateral tariff by $A$ on $B$ at the symmetric benchmark, $N\geq3$, and define
\begin{equation*}
H_N(\rho)\equiv\Big(1-\frac{mN}{N-1}\Big)(\rho-N\rho_D)-mN\,M_N(\rho).
\end{equation*}
For every untariffed bystander $c$, $d\log q_{Ac}/d\tau=H_N(\rho)/[N(N-1)M_N(\rho)]$, so wherever MML holds the real bilateral response has the sign of $H_N(\rho)$. Since $\rho_D$ does not depend on $\rho$, $dH_N/d\rho=1-mN$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If $m<1/N$, $H_N$ has the unique root
\begin{equation*}
\rho^*_q(N)=\frac{[(N-1)-mN]N\rho_D+mN[N\alpha_D(\eta-1)+1]}{(N-1)(1-mN)}.
\end{equation*}
Where MML holds, $A$ appreciates in real terms against $c$ for $\rho<\rho^*_q(N)$ and depreciates for $\rho>\rho^*_q(N)$; if MML holds at $\rho^*_q(N)$, then $\rho^*_q(N)>N\rho_D$.
\item If $m=1/N$, $H_N$ does not depend on $\rho$: $H_N=-[1+N\alpha_D(\eta-1)+N(N-2)\rho_D]/(N-1)$.
\item If $m>1/N$, $H_N$ is decreasing in $\rho$, so any crossing has the reverse orientation.
\item If $\eta\geq1$, so that MML holds for every $\rho>0$, and $m\geq1/N$, then $H_N(\rho)<0$ for every $\rho>0$: no positive $\rho$ produces a real depreciation against an untariffed bystander.
\item Under MML, if $m\leq(N-1)/N$, $A$'s REER appreciates. Under weak home bias this condition holds automatically, since $m=\alpha_T(1-\alpha_D)\leq(N-1)/N$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

At the US-like calibration and $N=3$, $m=0.08<1/3$ and $\rho^*_q=4.93$, against the nominal threshold $3\rho_D=3.96$. In the empirically relevant case $m<1/N$, a nominal bilateral reversal against bystanders is possible, the real reversal requires a higher $\rho$, and the effective rate, nominal and real, still appreciates.

The nested-CES model therefore gives economic content to the two components identified in \cref{sec:framework}. Average protection operates through the domestic diversion margin $\rho_D$, and relative treatment through cross-origin substitution $\rho$. Under a unilateral tariff, relative treatment overturns the conventional bilateral result against untariffed partners only when $\rho>N\rho_D$, while the effective exchange rate continues to appreciate, and in the low-import-share case a real bilateral reversal requires a still stronger cross-origin response. We next consider how these conclusions change when trading partners retaliate.
