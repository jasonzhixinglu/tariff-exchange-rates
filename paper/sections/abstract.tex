The textbook prediction that an import tariff appreciates the tariffing country's currency comes from a two-country world, in which the bilateral exchange rate, the effective exchange rate and the terms of trade coincide. With many countries they come apart. We characterize the long-run exchange-rate response to arbitrary tariff vectors in an $N$-country trade model. To first order, it is the inverse of an adjustment matrix applied to the tariff's trade-balance disturbance, and a multilateral Marshall--Lerner condition makes adjustment well behaved. Under partner symmetry, \emph{average protection} alone moves the effective exchange rate and \emph{relative treatment} alone moves bilateral rates around it.

In a nested-CES model two margins govern the responses: diversion toward the domestic economy, $\rho_D$, and substitution across foreign suppliers, $\rho$. A unilateral tariff always appreciates the effective exchange rate, yet depreciates the currency against every untariffed bystander when $\rho>N\rho_D$. Under retaliation the threshold falls to $\rho_D$, and both belligerents depreciate in effective terms. The symmetric economy reduces exactly to three countries. Under partner asymmetry, a uniform tariff still appreciates the currency against every partner and raises welfare, but a tariff on a small, low-weight partner can worsen the tariffing country's terms of trade and welfare when foreign suppliers are close substitutes. Read stylistically, the 2025 retaliation phase is consistent with the model, while the near-uniform phase shows none of the predicted effective appreciation.
