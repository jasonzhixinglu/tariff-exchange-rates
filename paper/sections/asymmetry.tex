\section{Asymmetry}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

The symmetric case delivers clean geometry: the NEER and the bilateral rates separate exactly, and thresholds are linear in $\rho$. Asymmetry breaks the eigen-structure. This section does not aim at a general theory of asymmetric trade networks. Its purpose is to show which intuitive conclusions of the symmetric case are structural and which are artefacts of symmetry. The most consequential casualty is the result that a unilateral tariff always improves the tariffing country's aggregate terms of trade.

Two weighted averages of bilateral responses appear in this section and should be kept apart. The \emph{effective exchange rate} is, as throughout the paper, the trade-weighted NEER of \cref{sec:decomposition}, with weights $w_{Aj}$ that combine $A$'s imports from and exports to $j$. The \emph{aggregate terms of trade} weight the same bilateral responses by $A$'s import shares $b_{Aj}$. At the symmetric benchmark the two weightings coincide; away from it they do not, and it is the import-weighted aggregate terms of trade that is welfare-relevant.

\subsection{The aggregate terms of trade and welfare}
\label{sec:welfare}

Let $b_{Aj}$ be partner $j$'s share in $A$'s import bundle, and $R^B_j\equiv\partial\nu_j/\partial\tau_{AB}$. Define the import-weighted foreign-price response
\begin{equation}
R_{A|B}\equiv\sum_{j\neq A}b_{Aj}R^B_j.
\label{eq:RAB}
\end{equation}
Under the producer-price normalization, a rise in $e_{Aj}$ raises the price of $j$'s good relative to $A$'s, so $A$'s aggregate terms of trade improve if and only if $R_{A|B}<0$.

\begin{lemma}[First-order welfare]
\label{lem:welfare}
At a free-trade baseline, for any tariff vector, the first-order change in $A$'s welfare, in units of income, is
\begin{equation}
\frac{dU_A}{U_A'\,I_A}=-\,m_A\sum_{j\neq A}b_{Aj}\,d\nu_j,
\label{eq:welfare}
\end{equation}
where $m_A$ is $A$'s import share of income. A marginal tariff thus raises $A$'s welfare if and only if it improves $A$'s aggregate terms of trade.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
$A$'s wage and non-tradable price are fixed in $A$'s currency. By the envelope theorem, $\lambda\,dU_A=dT^{rev}_A-\sum_jC_{Aj}\,dp_{Aj}$. At free trade $dT^{rev}_A=\sum_jf_{Aj}\,d\tau_{Aj}$ and $dp_{Aj}=p_{Aj}(d\nu_j+d\tau_{Aj})$. The tariff terms cancel, leaving $-\sum_jf_{Aj}d\nu_j$. Foreign tariffs affect $A$ only through $d\nu$. The full proof is in \cref{app:proofs}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The welfare gradient.} By \cref{lem:welfare}, the first-order welfare effect is linear in the tariff vector. For tariffs imposed by $A$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dU_A}{U_A'\,I_A}=\sum_{j\neq A}g_j\,d\tau_{Aj},\qquad g_j\equiv-m_A\,R_{A|j},
\label{eq:gradient}
\end{equation}
where $R_{A|j}$ is the import-weighted response \cref{eq:RAB} to a tariff on $j$ alone. The component $g_j$ is the welfare gain from a marginal tariff on partner $j$, and its sign is that of the aggregate terms-of-trade effect of that tariff. A uniform marginal tariff, $d\tau_{Aj}=dt$ for all $j$, is worth $\sum_jg_j\,dt$.

\begin{proposition}[Uniform tariffs]
\label{prop:uniform}
Consider any balanced free-trade baseline at which MML holds; country sizes, origin shares, home biases and parameters may differ across countries. A uniform marginal tariff by $A$, $d\tau_{Aj}=dt>0$ for all $j\neq A$, generates the disturbance
\begin{equation}
F_j=-f_{Aj}\,\rho_D^A\,dt,\qquad \rho_D^A\equiv\eta_Ah_A+(1-h_A)(1-\alpha_T^A)>0,
\label{eq:uniformF}
\end{equation}
where $h_A$ is $A$'s home share of tradable spending and $\eta_A$, $\alpha_T^A$ are $A$'s own parameters. The tariff appreciates $A$'s currency weakly against every partner, and strictly against every partner if $A$ imports from all of them or $J$ is irreducible. It therefore improves $A$'s aggregate terms of trade and raises $A$'s welfare to first order: $\sum_{j\neq A}g_j>0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
At unchanged exchange rates $A$'s tariffs move only the flows $f_{Aj}$ (\cref{eq:linear}). A uniform tariff raises every import price by the same amount, so relative import prices do not change and the cross-origin margin $(1-\rho)$ drops out. Every import share then moves by $(1-\eta_A)h_A\,dt$ and $A$'s income by $m_A\,dt$, which gives \cref{eq:uniformF}. Since $F<0$, \cref{eq:signrule} gives $d\nu\leq0$, and \cref{lem:welfare} gives the welfare gain. The full proof is in \cref{app:proofs}.
\end{proof}

At the symmetric benchmark $\rho_D^A=\rho_D$ and $f_{Aj}=m/(N-1)$, so \cref{eq:uniformF} is the average-protection disturbance of \cref{sec:theorem}. The proposition extends the conventional appreciation result from that benchmark to \emph{any} balanced baseline. Every partner's trade balance is disturbed in the same direction, because a uniform tariff switches off cross-origin substitution, the force behind every reversal in this paper, and leaves only domestic diversion. Since $F\leq0$ componentwise, \cref{eq:signrule} gives $d\nu\leq0$ componentwise: disturbances of one sign cannot produce responses of the opposite sign. The replication code confirms \cref{eq:uniformF} to $10^{-12}$, and the signs of $F$, $d\nu$ and the welfare change, on random asymmetric baselines with country-specific parameters and $N=3,4,5$.

What the proposition does not say is that each component of the gradient is positive. A tariff on a single partner $B$ does change relative import prices, and $R_{A|B}$ combines the favorable move against $B$ with the moves against everyone else, which can be adverse. \Cref{sec:totcondition,sec:threecountry} show that $g_B<0$ is possible. Since the components sum to a positive number, with $N=3$ the condition $g_B<0$ implies $g_C>0$: whenever a tariff on the target lowers welfare, a tariff on the bystander raises it.

At the symmetric benchmark the components are equal, so each is positive. Import weights and trade weights both equal $1/(N-1)$ there, so $R_{A|B}=d\nu^E_A/d\tau<0$ (\cref{prop:unilateral}). \textbf{At the symmetric benchmark a unilateral tariff on one partner improves the tariffing country's aggregate terms of trade and raises its welfare to first order, even when it depreciates against every bystander.} Under bilateral retaliation the sign flips when $\rho>\rho_D$. For $N=3$,
\begin{equation*}
\frac{R^B_B+R^B_C}{2}=-\frac{\rho_D}{2M_3}:
\end{equation*}
the favorable move against the target always dominates the adverse move against the bystander when weights are equal.

\paragraph{Relation to optimal-tariff theory.} A country with market power gains from a small tariff. This classic terms-of-trade argument is the starting point of \citet{johnson1953}, who shows that a country with sufficient monopoly--monopsony power can remain better off than under free trade even after foreign retaliation. The argument has empirical support: \citet{broda2008} find that countries not bound by WTO commitments set higher tariffs on goods whose foreign export supply is less elastic. \citet{costinot2015} show, in a two-country Ricardian model with a continuum of goods, that optimal import tariffs are uniform across goods. \Cref{prop:uniform} recovers the classic result for a small \emph{uniform} tariff at any asymmetric baseline under MML, with the terms-of-trade gain delivered through the exchange rate. What fails is the partner-by-partner version. With many partners, a tariff on one of them also moves the terms of trade against the others. Nondiscrimination is what confines a country's tariffs to a single terms-of-trade channel \citep{bagwell1999,bagwell2002}. Our results show a cost of discrimination from the imposing country's own point of view: even though $A$ has market power against every partner, a tariff on a single partner can lower its welfare.

\subsection{The aggregate terms-of-trade condition}
\label{sec:totcondition}

Separate the target from the remaining suppliers, and let $\bar R^B_{-B}=\sum_{j\neq A,B}b_{Aj}R^B_j/(1-b_{AB})$ be the conditional import-weighted bystander response. Then $R_{A|B}=b_{AB}R^B_B+(1-b_{AB})\bar R^B_{-B}$.

\begin{definition}[Aggregate terms-of-trade condition]
\label{def:tot}
Unilateral tariffs by $A$ have the conventional aggregate terms-of-trade effect if, for every target $B$,
\begin{equation}
b_{AB}R^B_B+(1-b_{AB})\bar R^B_{-B}<0.
\label{eq:totcondition}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

If $R^B_B<0$ and $\bar R^B_{-B}>0$, the condition fails if and only if
\begin{equation}
\frac{\bar R^B_{-B}}{-R^B_B}>\frac{b_{AB}}{1-b_{AB}}.
\label{eq:totfail}
\end{equation}
The left side is equilibrium incidence: the adverse bystander move relative to the favorable move against the target. The right side is exposure: $A$'s import dependence on the target relative to other suppliers. A bilateral bystander reversal is therefore \textbf{necessary but not sufficient} for an aggregate reversal. The adverse moves must also be large enough and carry enough weight in $A$'s basket.

In terms of \cref{eq:gradient}, the condition requires every component $g_B$ of the welfare gradient to be positive, whereas \cref{prop:uniform} guarantees only their sum. The condition has a three-country form for any configuration of bystanders simply because $\bar R^B_{-B}$ is defined as their import-weighted average; what does not carry over under heterogeneous bystanders is the representation of the bloc as an ordinary country with primitive parameters (\cref{sec:heterogeneous}).

\subsection{Asymmetric partners in the three-country model}
\label{sec:threecountry}

In the three-country model the local system is $A(\rho)R=F(\rho)$ with $A\equiv-J$. At a given baseline every entry of $A$ and $F$ is affine in $\rho$, because $\rho$ enters share responses only through $(1-\rho)$. Since $A$ is $2\times2$, its adjugate is affine too, and
\begin{equation}
R_{A|B}(\rho)=\frac{b'\adj(A(\rho))F(\rho)}{\det A(\rho)},\qquad
b'\adj(A(\rho))F(\rho)=q_2\rho^2+q_1\rho+q_0\equiv Q_{A|B}(\rho).
\label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
Under MML $\det A>0$, so the aggregate condition is $Q_{A|B}(\rho)<0$. The same structure governs the bilateral bystander response, whose numerator has leading coefficient
\begin{equation*}
\alpha_T^2\,b_{AB}b_{AC}b_{CA}b_{CB}(1-h_A)(1-h_C)y_Ay_C>0
\end{equation*}
(with a common $\alpha_T$), where $h_i$ are home shares of tradable spending and $y_i$ incomes.

\begin{proposition}[Three qualitative cases]
\label{prop:threecases}
Holding the baseline fixed:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \textbf{Symmetric:} $q_2=0$ and $Q<0$ for all $\rho$. The condition always holds.
\item \textbf{$q_2>0$, $q_0<0$:} exactly one positive root $\rho^*$. The condition holds for $\rho<\rho^*$ and fails for $\rho>\rho^*$.
\item \textbf{$q_2<0$, $q_1>0$, $q_0<0$, positive discriminant:} two positive roots. The condition fails only for intermediate $\rho$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Examples.} All use $\alpha_D=0.8$, $\alpha_T=0.4$, $\eta=1.5$ unless stated, with the free-trade baseline computed from primitives at $\bar\rho=3\rho_D=3.96$ and then held fixed; $\hat Q$ denotes $Q$ normalized by $|q_2|$ (or by $|q_1|$ when $q_2=0$).
\begin{itemize}
\item[(a)] Symmetric benchmark: $\hat Q=-(\rho+2.2)$.
\item[(b)] Labor endowments $(L_A,L_B,L_C)=(1,0.22,1)$, $\beta_{AB}=0.2$, $\beta_{AC}=0.8$, other origin weights symmetric: $\hat Q=\rho^2-2.6695\rho-2.2568$, so $\rho^*=3.344$.
\item[(c)] A strongly asymmetric network, specified directly in baseline shares: $\alpha_T=0.862$, $\eta=3.007$, home shares $(0.201,0.854,0.360)$, origin shares $(b_{AB},b_{AC})=(0.525,0.475)$, $(b_{BA},b_{BC})=(0.766,0.234)$, $(b_{CA},b_{CB})=(0.407,0.593)$, and incomes $(1,5.126,0.867)$, which balance trade at these shares. Then $\hat Q=-\rho^2+31.53\rho-198.04$, so the condition fails for $8.66<\rho<22.86$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Takeaway.} The aggregate terms of trade, and hence the first-order welfare gain from a tariff, are most likely to \emph{deteriorate} when three forces align:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the target is small relative to the untariffed suppliers;
\item the target has a small weight in the tariffing country's imports;
\item foreign suppliers are close substitutes (high $\rho$).
\end{enumerate}
In that region the bystander depreciation is both large and heavily weighted, while the gain against the target is small and lightly weighted. \textbf{A country can lower its own welfare by tariffing a small partner whose goods are easily replaced by others'.} This happens not because the tariff fails to divert expenditure, but because the diversion is so complete that the price of the replacements rises. By \cref{prop:uniform}, the same country gains from a uniform tariff at the same baseline. In the ``$+$'' cells of \cref{tab:q2}, $g_B<0$ beyond the root, so $g_C>0$ there: tariffing the bystander instead would pay. \textbf{The gains from protection are guaranteed only for uniform protection; discrimination can backfire.} The two-root case requires richer network asymmetry: in a representative example, $L_B/L_C=2$, $\beta_{AB}=0.2$, $\beta_{BA}=0.9$ and $\beta_{CA}=0.1$, the condition fails for $6.29<\rho<15.03$.

\paragraph{The trade-weighted NEER.} The same logic applies to the NEER with trade weights. When the bystander carries most of $A$'s trade, the NEER can depreciate under a \emph{unilateral} tariff. \Cref{tab:neer} fixes $\beta_{AB}=0.2$ and sets $L_B$ so that $w_{AC}$ takes the stated value. Its last column shows that the trade-weighted and import-weighted thresholds differ substantially in the same economy. In the second row, whose primitives are nearly those of example (b), the NEER depreciates only for $\rho>3.97$, whereas the aggregate terms of trade deteriorate for $\rho>3.40$ (3.34 at the fixed baseline of example (b)). The gap is a matter of weighting, not of method. $A$ buys little from $B$ but $B$ buys a good deal from $A$, so the target's weight in $A$'s trade ($w_{AB}=0.15$) exceeds its weight in $A$'s imports ($b_{AB}=0.095$). The favorable move against the target therefore counts for more in the NEER than in the welfare-relevant terms of trade.

\paragraph{Other casualties of asymmetry} (\cref{app:departures}).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Thresholds move.} Near the symmetric point the bystander threshold rises with the target's import weight ($d\rho^*/d\beta_{AB}=+3.70$), with its size ($+0.99$) and with $A$'s home bias ($+5.31$), and falls slightly with bystander size ($-0.20$), at the calibration $\alpha_D=0.7$, $\alpha_T=0.6$, $\eta=1.5$ of \cref{fig:geometry}; the US-like values have the same signs (\cref{tab:sens}).
\item \emph{Thresholds can disappear.} With primitives rather than shares held fixed, a higher $\rho$ also shifts baseline shares toward the cheapest origin, and a large target can crowd the bystander out of $A$'s imports. At the same calibration, with $L_B=2.2$ the bystander reverses only for $5.99<\rho<53.8$, and with $L_B\geq2.6$ it never reverses for $\rho\leq1000$.
\item \emph{The ordering $d\nu_B<d\nu_C$ can fail}, but in every case found only with $\rho<\eta$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Heterogeneous bystanders}
\label{sec:heterogeneous}

With asymmetric bystanders the composite of \cref{prop:composite} no longer exists. Partition the system into the target and the remaining countries $R$. With $q$ denoting $A$'s import weights within $R$,
\begin{equation}
d\nu^A_C\equiv q'd\nu_R=-q'J_{RR}^{-1}J_{RB}\,d\nu_B-q'J_{RR}^{-1}F_R\,d\tau,
\label{eq:hetero}
\end{equation}
which depends on the full internal adjustment structure of the bloc. The aggregate terms-of-trade condition still has three-country form, by the definition of $\bar R^B_{-B}$, but the bloc cannot generally be represented by a country with primitive parameters. Reversal and the failure of aggregation share an origin. The nested structure that lets $\rho$ direct expenditure toward other foreign suppliers is exactly what makes it matter \emph{which} suppliers receive it. With a single, unnested elasticity the bloc aggregates exactly, but bystander reversal disappears.

\subsection{A stylized reading of the 2025 episode}
\label{sec:2025}

We do not calibrate. A static, long-run, balanced-trade model compared against monthly exchange-rate data is not a test, and \cref{sec:threecountry} shows that the answer under realistic asymmetry depends on network details that a three-country stylization cannot capture. We therefore ask only which way each feature of the 2025 episode pushes the trade channel, and we apply the same standard in both directions: a movement is \emph{consistent with} the trade channel when the model predicts its sign in the relevant configuration, and \emph{not explained by} the trade channel when the model predicts the opposite sign for every admissible $\rho$. Neither statement attributes the movement to its cause. The rule applies to a phase only insofar as the phase approximates a single configuration of the model, and in 2025 none did cleanly: each phase mixed discriminatory, retaliatory and near-uniform elements. \Cref{fig:episode} shows the exchange rates and the tariff timeline, and \cref{tab:phases} reports the change within each phase. Exchange-rate changes are measured relative to the January~2025 average, before the first tariff order of February~1, and signed so that a positive number is a dollar depreciation. The model's effective exchange rate corresponds to the full broad dollar index, which includes China; the index excluding China isolates the bystanders.

\paragraph{Phase 1: unilateral tariffs on China (February~1--April~2).} The United States raised tariffs on China by 20 percentage points in two steps, effective February~4 and March~4. China's countermeasures in this phase were product-specific rather than across the board, and there was no general US tariff on third countries. The phase was not a clean unilateral experiment, however. US tariffs of 25 percent on Canada and Mexico took effect on March~4, and Canada retaliated the same day with a 25 percent surtax on C\$30 billion of US goods \citep{canadafinance2025}; Canada and Mexico together carry $27.6$ percent of the broad index. The configuration mixed a unilateral, discriminatory tariff on China with a retaliatory leg in North America. The US--China leg behaved as predicted: the dollar was roughly unchanged against the renminbi ($-0.3$ percent on daily data, $+0.6$ percent on monthly averages), with no sign of a depreciation against the target. Against bystanders the dollar weakened, by $4.5$ percent against the euro and $1.9$ percent against the broad index excluding China from the end of January to April~2, which is consistent with the trade channel if foreign suppliers are close substitutes ($\rho>N\rho_D$). Against the full broad index, which includes China, it weakened by $1.7$ percent. A purely unilateral tariff predicts effective appreciation, but retaliation predicts effective depreciation once $\rho>\rho_D$ (\cref{prop:retaliation}), so in this mixed phase the effective move is not a test of either. Nearly all of the phase's effective movement came in the week of March~3--7 ($1.8$ percent against the broad index), which combined the North American tariffs and Canadian retaliation with the announcement by Germany's prospective governing parties of a large defence and infrastructure package and a loosening of the debt brake \citep{ripper2025}. In that week the dollar fell by $4.4$ percent against the euro, $1.3$ percent against the peso and $0.3$ percent against the Canadian dollar. The data do not separate these forces.

\paragraph{Phase 2: retaliation (April~3--May~12).} China retaliated nearly one-for-one (US 145 percent, China 125 percent), while the United States imposed only a 10 percent baseline tariff on others. The configuration moves toward bilateral retaliation, and the bystander threshold falls from about $N\rho_D$ to $\rho_D$:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Retaliation points to dollar depreciation against bystanders.} At US-like parameters $\rho_D\approx1.3$, below almost all cross-origin estimates. The model predicts a roughly stable USD/RMB rate together with dollar weakness against bystanders and in effective terms. That is the pattern of the phase: the dollar moved little against the renminbi ($+0.9$ percent from April~2 to May~12, $-0.7$ percent between the March and April averages), but fell by $2.2$--$3.9$ percent against the euro and by $2.0$--$2.5$ percent against the broad index excluding China (\cref{tab:phases}). It is also the event-study regularity of \citet{ostry2025}.
\item \emph{Expected retaliation works the same way, as a matter of interpretation.} Our model is static, so expectations enter only through the tariff structure agents expect to persist. If markets price the long-run configuration as retaliatory, the relevant comparative static is \cref{prop:retaliation}, not \cref{prop:unilateral}. In a forward-looking extension, credible threats of retaliation would then move exchange rates before retaliation occurs. This is an interpretation, not a result of the model.
\item \emph{High cross-origin substitutability points the same way.} Trade between China and other suppliers, especially Vietnam, Mexico and other Asian producers, reallocated quickly \citep{fajgelbaum2024,freund2024,alfaro2023}. That is the high-$\rho$ environment in which diversion is strong.
\item \emph{China's size does not.} China is large in absolute terms and a substantial US supplier. Near the symmetric point, a larger target and a larger import weight on the target both \emph{raise} the bystander threshold (\cref{sec:threecountry}). They also move the aggregate terms-of-trade condition toward its conventional sign.\footnote{Placed in the grid of \cref{tab:q2} with 2024 data (US imports by origin from the US Census Bureau, China's from its customs statistics, the rest of the world's trade with both as residuals, and sizes from PPP GDP), a US--China--rest-of-world configuration falls in the region where $q_2>0$. In a three-country network calibrated to the observed import-origin shares, the aggregate terms-of-trade condition for a US tariff on China fails only for $\rho$ above about $4.5$--$4.7$, while the bilateral bystander threshold is about $3.9$--$4.0$; the ranges span a common US-like home bias and the measured US home share. The bystander threshold lies at the upper end of the central range of estimates, $\rho\in[2,4]$, and the aggregate threshold just above it. The calibration imposes a balanced baseline, ignores gaps between mirror trade statistics and uses model-implied home shares for China and the rest of the world. It places the episode on the map; it is not a calibration of exchange rates.} The configuration is therefore not clear-cut. Retaliation and $\rho$ lean toward dollar depreciation against bystanders, while China's weight leans against it. Where the balance falls depends on bilateral shares and sizes that a three-country stylization summarizes too crudely.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Phase 3: near-uniform tariffs (August~7--December~31).} After the Geneva truce (effective May~14) the US tariff on China was 30 percent, falling to 20 percent on November~10, and China kept a 10 percent tariff on all US goods. Under the framework agreements in force from August~7, US tariffs were 15 percent on the EU and 10--20 percent on most others. Relative treatment had narrowed but not disappeared. Until November~10, China faced 30 percent against 10--20 percent for most others, and China's retaliation remained in place; in North America, the US rate on non-USMCA Canadian goods rose to 35 percent on August~1, and Canada's countermeasures on steel, aluminium and autos stayed in force. For the uniform component the relevant experiment is average protection, which predicts mild dollar appreciation in effective terms (\cref{prop:uniform}). The residual tariff gaps, met by retaliation, are retaliation configurations in miniature, and they lean toward effective depreciation if $\rho>\rho_D$. Within the phase the dollar showed no sign of the predicted appreciation: the broad index barely moved ($-0.1$ to $+0.6$ percent, \cref{tab:phases}). In levels, the dollar in December was $13.1$ percent weaker against the euro, $7.2$ percent weaker against the broad index and $7.7$ percent weaker against the index excluding China than in January. That level sums all phases, including retaliation, and most of it built up before August: from Geneva to August~6 alone the dollar fell by a further $4.9$ percent against the euro and $2.5$ percent against the broad index. Forces outside the model that plausibly matter for the level include risk premia and tariff uncertainty, the erosion of convenience yields, valuation effects, monetary policy and expectations of renewed escalation.

\paragraph{Vietnam} illustrates the classification point. The dollar strengthened against the dong through 2025, by $3.2$ percent between January and December, nearly all of it by July, which looks inconsistent with a bystander-depreciation story. But Vietnam was itself a principal target: a 46 percent rate was announced in April, and a 20 percent rate applied from August~7. From August~7 to November~10, Vietnam's 20 percent against China's 30 percent was relative treatment in Vietnam's favour, a bystander-like position in which strong diversion leans toward dollar weakness against the dong. Only after November~10, when both faced 20 percent, was there little relative treatment between them, so that the model predicts the conventional own-tariff result. Within the near-uniform phase the rate was roughly flat ($0.0$ to $+0.5$ percent).

\paragraph{What a quantitative general trade model would need.} The analysis says what to look for in richer models. Retaliation lowers the threshold for multilateral depreciation. High cross-origin substitutability raises the diversion force. Large, heavily weighted targets work against both. Near-uniform tariffs collapse the problem back to average protection. Quantitative multi-country models with input--output linkages \citep[e.g.,][]{ignatenko2025,darracqparies2026} can contain all of these features. Our results identify which combinations of them determine the sign.
