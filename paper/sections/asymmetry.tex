\section{Asymmetry}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

Partner symmetry makes the decomposition between average protection and relative treatment especially transparent. We now ask how the central results change when countries differ in size, trade shares, and preferences. The main distinction is between uniform and discriminatory protection. Uniform protection retains the conventional local aggregate result under general asymmetry. Discriminatory protection need not: when the target is small or lightly weighted and cross-origin substitution is sufficiently strong, adverse movements against larger bystanders can dominate the favorable movement against the target.

\subsection{Uniform and Discriminatory Protection}

Let $b_{Aj}$ denote partner $j$'s share in $A$'s baseline import expenditure, and let $R^B_j\equiv\partial\nu_j/\partial\tau_{AB}$ be the response of $A$'s bilateral rate against $j$ to a marginal tariff on $B$. Define
\begin{equation}
R_{A|B}\equiv\sum_{j\neq A}b_{Aj}R^B_j.
\end{equation}
Because producer prices are fixed in their own currencies, a tariff on $B$ improves $A$'s import-weighted aggregate terms of trade if and only if $R_{A|B}<0$. The welfare result is stated at a balanced free-trade baseline of the model of \cref{sec:model} with interior shares, lump-sum rebate of tariff revenue, and exchange rates differentiable in tariffs, which holds whenever $J$ is nonsingular.

\begin{lemma}[First-order welfare]
\label{lem:welfare}
Under these assumptions, let $d\tau$ be any marginal tariff vector, imposed by $A$ or by other countries. To first order, the change in the welfare of $A$'s representative household, in units of $A$'s income, is
\begin{equation}
\frac{dU_A}{U_A'\,I_A}=-\,m_A\sum_{j\neq A}b_{Aj}\,d\nu_j,
\end{equation}
where $m_A$ is $A$'s import share of income. A marginal tariff change therefore raises $A$'s own welfare to first order if and only if it improves $A$'s import-weighted aggregate terms of trade.
\end{lemma}

\noindent At free trade, the first-order tariff-revenue gain offsets the direct increase in import prices; the proof is in \cref{app:proofs}.

\begin{proposition}[Uniform protection]
\label{prop:uniform}
At any balanced free-trade baseline satisfying MML and the assumptions of \cref{lem:welfare}, with country sizes, origin shares, home biases and parameters free to differ, a marginal uniform tariff by $A$ on all partners, $d\tau_{Aj}=dt>0$ for all $j\neq A$, appreciates $A$'s currency weakly against every partner, strictly if $A$ imports from all of them or $J$ is irreducible, improves $A$'s import-weighted aggregate terms of trade, and raises $A$'s welfare to first order.
\end{proposition}

Uniform protection leaves relative import prices across foreign suppliers unchanged. Cross-origin substitution therefore creates no differential incidence across partners. Under MML, the tariffing country appreciates against every partner, its aggregate terms of trade improve, and its welfare rises to first order. Asymmetry alone therefore does not overturn the conventional local result. The possibility of reversal arises from discriminatory protection.

Now let $A$ tariff a single target $B$, and define the import-weighted average response against the remaining suppliers as $\bar R^B_{-B}\equiv\sum_{j\neq A,B}\frac{b_{Aj}}{1-b_{AB}}R^B_j$. Then
\begin{equation}
R_{A|B}=b_{AB}R^B_B+(1-b_{AB})\bar R^B_{-B}.
\label{eq:totsplit}
\end{equation}
If the tariffing country appreciates against the target but depreciates on average against the bystanders, its aggregate terms of trade deteriorate only when the adverse bystander movement is sufficiently large relative to the favorable target movement and the bystanders carry sufficient weight in the import basket. A bilateral bystander reversal is therefore necessary but not sufficient for an aggregate reversal.

\subsection{A Small Target and Large Bystanders}

The economically relevant asymmetric case is transparent from the weighted-incidence condition. Suppose the tariff target is small or carries a low weight in the tariffing country's imports, while the untariffed bystanders account for a correspondingly large share of trade. An appreciation against the target then contributes relatively little to the aggregate import price, while depreciation against the bystanders receives greater weight. Strong cross-origin substitution reinforces this asymmetry by redirecting expenditure toward the larger untariffed suppliers.

\begin{proposition}[Aggregate reversal under discriminatory protection]
\label{prop:reversal}
Fix a balanced free-trade baseline satisfying MML. Under asymmetry, a marginal discriminatory tariff can worsen the tariffing country's aggregate terms of trade and reduce its welfare to first order.
\end{proposition}

In the numerical comparative statics, reversals arise most readily when the target is small or carries a low weight in the tariffing country's imports and foreign suppliers are sufficiently close substitutes. The mechanism is a weighted-incidence effect: the favorable movement against the target receives little weight, while the adverse movements against larger bystanders receive more. As $\rho$ rises, stronger substitution toward those suppliers can make the latter movements dominate (\cref{fig:tot}).
