\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

With two countries, a tariff moves a single exchange rate, and the bilateral rate, the effective rate and the terms of trade coincide. With three or more countries they separate. The general framework writes every bilateral response as a multilateral adjustment matrix applied to the vector of tariff-induced trade-balance disturbances. The multilateral Marshall--Lerner condition makes that adjustment stable but does not determine the sign of every bilateral response (\cref{sec:framework}).

Under partner symmetry, any tariff vector separates into average protection, which moves the effective rate, and relative treatment, which moves bilateral rates around it (\cref{prop:decomposition}). The nested-CES model maps these components into the domestic diversion margin $\rho_D$ and cross-origin substitution $\rho$. Locally, at the symmetric balanced free-trade benchmark and under MML, a unilateral tariff appreciates the tariffing country's effective rate but depreciates its currency against untariffed bystanders when $\rho>N\rho_D$ (\cref{prop:unilateral}); retaliation lowers the relevant threshold to $\rho_D$ (\cref{prop:retaliation}).

Under strong asymmetry the reversal can extend to the import-weighted aggregate terms of trade and to own welfare: a discriminatory marginal tariff can lower the tariffing country's welfare to first order when the target is small, carries a low weight in its imports, and foreign suppliers are close substitutes (\cref{sec:totcondition,sec:threecountry}). Uniform protection, by contrast, retains the conventional local result under the conditions of \cref{prop:uniform}.

Applied to 2025, the framework classifies tariff configurations by the exchange-rate signs they imply; it does not identify causes (\cref{sec:2025}). A quantitative multi-country network model, with measured elasticities, trade weights and retaliation structure, is the natural next step.
