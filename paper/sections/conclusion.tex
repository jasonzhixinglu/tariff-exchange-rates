\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In a two-country world, the bilateral exchange rate, the effective exchange rate and the terms of trade coincide, and a tariff appreciates the tariffing country's currency wherever Marshall--Lerner holds. In a multilateral world they separate. We have characterized what the real trade channel implies once they do.

The general response is the inverse of a multilateral adjustment matrix applied to a vector of tariff disturbances. A multilateral Marshall--Lerner condition makes adjustment well behaved without fixing the sign of any bilateral response. Under symmetry, average protection moves the effective rate and relative treatment moves bilateral rates around it. In a nested-CES model the conventional result survives exactly at the effective level under unilateral tariffs. It fails bilaterally against bystanders when cross-origin substitution exceeds $N$ times the domestic diversion margin. An exact three-country representation shows that the role of $N$ comes from the inward-looking, highly substitutable trade of the bystander bloc, not from its size. Under bilateral retaliation the belligerents' bilateral rate is unchanged, and once cross-origin substitution exceeds the domestic diversion margin itself each belligerent depreciates against bystanders and in effective terms; only in a multilateral tariff war does the tariffing country depreciate against everyone. Under asymmetry, even the effective and welfare-relevant conclusions can fail for discriminatory tariffs: tariffing a small, lightly weighted partner whose goods are easily replaced can worsen the tariffing country's aggregate terms of trade. A uniform tariff, by contrast, appreciates the currency against every partner and raises welfare at any baseline under MML. The gains from protection are guaranteed only for uniform protection.

The implications run both ways. Appreciation against a tariffed partner is not evidence of broad currency strength. A multilateral depreciation during a discriminatory or retaliatory tariff episode can be consistent with the trade channel, so the two-country prediction alone does not show that financial forces were at work. Conversely, in a unilateral or nearly uniform configuration the trade channel predicts effective appreciation, so effective weakness in such a configuration is not explained by it. Whether an episode is such a configuration has to be checked phase by phase, because actual episodes mix unilateral, retaliatory and near-uniform elements.

Natural next steps are to embed the decomposition in a quantitative multi-country model with input--output linkages and heterogeneous elasticities, and to add nominal rigidities, invoicing and a financial account. This would let the long-run real benchmark derived here interact with the short-run monetary and portfolio dynamics that dominate exchange rates at business-cycle frequencies.
