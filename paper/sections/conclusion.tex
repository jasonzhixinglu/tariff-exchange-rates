\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

With two countries, a tariff moves a single exchange rate, so the bilateral exchange rate, the effective exchange rate, and the long-run terms of trade coincide. In a multilateral economy these objects separate. Tariffs change both average protection and relative treatment across foreign suppliers, and the resulting exchange-rate adjustment depends on the full vector of trade-balance disturbances rather than on the tariffing country's bilateral relationship with the target alone.

This distinction changes the conventional tariff result. Under symmetry, a unilateral tariff appreciates the tariffing country's effective exchange rate but can depreciate its currency against untariffed bystanders when cross-origin substitution is sufficiently strong relative to domestic diversion. Retaliation strengthens the relative-treatment force and lowers the bystander threshold. Under asymmetry, uniform protection retains the conventional local aggregate terms-of-trade and welfare result, while discriminatory protection can overturn it when the target is small or lightly weighted and adverse movements against larger bystanders dominate.

The 2025 episode illustrates why the cross-country configuration of protection matters, but the sign comparisons do not identify the trade mechanism or its quantitative contribution. The broader implication is that the effect of tariffs on ``the exchange rate'' is not well defined without specifying both the exchange-rate object and the configuration of tariffs across trading partners.
