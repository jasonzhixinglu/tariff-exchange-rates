\section{Away from the Symmetric Free-Trade Benchmark}
\label{app:departures}

The results of \cref{sec:decomposition,sec:nestedces,sec:tradewars} are local to the symmetric free-trade benchmark. This appendix examines four departures from it: a positive common external tariff, finite tariff changes, asymmetric primitives, and the trade-weighted effective rate. The representation \cref{eq:response} and the MML condition apply at any balanced baseline. What changes is the mapping from primitives to the eigenvalues of \cref{prop:decomposition} and, once partner symmetry fails, whether the common and zero-sum directions remain eigen-directions at all. In every case the baseline equilibrium is recomputed from primitives rather than by perturbing shares, and MML holds at every point reported. Unless stated otherwise, numerical values use the calibration of \cref{fig:geometry}, $\alpha_D=0.7$, $\alpha_T=0.6$ and $\eta=1.5$ ($\rho_D=1.17$); US-like values are reported where they are discussed in the main text.

\subsection{A positive common external tariff}
\label{app:asym-tariff}

Suppose $A$ levies a common tariff $\tau_0$ on all $N-1$ partners, which impose no tariffs of their own. This baseline treats the partners alike, so partner symmetry holds and \cref{prop:decomposition} applies. Relative to free trade, $A$'s currency appreciates by the same amount against every partner, so $A$'s goods are more expensive abroad and baseline shares differ from their free-trade values. We measure tariff changes as $t_j=d\log(1+\tau_{Aj})$, which coincides with $t_j=d\tau_{Aj}$ at free trade. Let $\theta\equiv\tau_0/(1+\tau_0)$ denote the tariff wedge, $\mu_A$ the import share of $A$'s tradable expenditure, $\mu_P$ the import share of a partner's tradable expenditure, and $\omega$ the share of $A$'s good in a partner's import bundle. The rebate channel enters through $\phi\equiv\alpha_T\mu_A/(1-\theta\alpha_T\mu_A)$, the response of $A$'s income to its import expenditure. Trade between $A$ and each partner is balanced at the baseline, so $X\equiv f_{Aj}=f_{jA}$ denotes this bilateral flow, and $Y\equiv f_{jk}$ the flow between any two partners. Linearizing at this baseline gives
\begin{equation}
g_S=-X\rho_D(\tau_0),\qquad g_D=-X\rho,\qquad j_S=XM_S,\qquad j_D=\big(X+(N-1)Y\big)M_D,
\label{eq:tau0eigen}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align*}
\rho_D(\tau_0)&=1-\phi(1-\theta)+(\eta-1)(1-\mu_A)(1+\phi\theta),\\
M_S&=1+(\eta-1)(1-\mu_A)(1+\phi\theta)+(\rho-1)(1-\omega)+(\eta-1)(1-\mu_P)\,\omega,\\
M_D&=1+(\rho-1)\frac{N-3+\omega}{N-2}+(\eta-1)(1-\mu_P)\frac{N-1-\omega}{N-2}.
\end{align*}
At free trade $\theta=0$, $\phi=m$, $\mu_A=\mu_P=1-\alpha_D$ and $\omega=1/(N-1)$, so $\rho_D(0)=\rho_D$ and $M_S=M_D=M_N$.

The eigenvalues in \cref{eq:tau0eigen} differ in the trade flows on which they act. Tariff disturbances along both components fall only on partners' trade with $A$, so $g_S$ and $g_D$ are proportional to $X$. A common exchange-rate movement leaves cross rates among partners unchanged and also shifts only partners' trade with $A$, so $j_S$ is proportional to $X$, and $X$ cancels from $\lambda_A=-\rho_D(\tau_0)/M_S$. A relative movement also shifts trade among partners. Under a zero-sum movement $v$, partner $j$'s rate moves by $v_j$ against $A$ and by $v_j-v_k$ against partner $k$; since $\sum_{k\neq j}v_k=-v_j$, its average movement against the other partners is $(N-1)/(N-2)$ times $v_j$. The base $X+(N-1)Y$ is therefore partner $j$'s trade with $A$ plus its trade with the other partners, the latter weighted by this factor. Define $x\equiv X/(X+(N-1)Y)$, $A$'s share of the trade on which relative exchange-rate movements act. Then $\lambda_R=-x\rho/M_D$ and $j_D/j_S=(1/x)(M_D/M_S)$. At free trade $X=Y$, so $x=1/N$ and $j_D=Nj_S$: the factor $N$ in the bystander threshold is $1/x$ evaluated at the free-trade baseline.

For a further tariff on $B$, $t=dt\,e_B$, the bystander response is $d\nu_C=(\lambda_A-\lambda_R)\,dt/(N-1)$, so $A$ depreciates against $C$ if and only if
\begin{equation}
\rho>\rho^*(\tau_0)\equiv\frac{\rho_D(\tau_0)}{x}\,\frac{M_D}{M_S}.
\label{eq:tau0thresh}
\end{equation}
The condition is implicit in $\rho$, because $\omega$, $\mu_P$, $x$ and $M_S$ depend on baseline shares that themselves depend on $\rho$; we compute it by root-finding with the baseline re-solved at each $\rho$. Since the response is linear in $t$, a marginal preferential cut for $B$, $t=-dt\,e_B$, worsens $C$'s terms of trade under the same condition. \Cref{tab:tau0} reports the threshold relative to its free-trade value.

The threshold rises with $\tau_0$, mainly because $x$ falls. The common appreciation makes $A$'s goods more expensive, so $A$'s share of each partner's imports falls. At $N=3$ and $\tau_0=1$, $\omega$ falls from $1/2$ to $0.29$ and $x$ from $1/3$ to $0.17$. Relative treatment then disturbs a smaller part of the trade on which relative exchange-rate movements act. A rise in $M_S$ relative to $M_D$ offsets part of this effect. The local threshold also understates what a discrete preferential cut requires. In the exact model at $N=3$, removing the tariff on $B$ from $\tau_0=0.5$ worsens $C$'s terms of trade only if $\rho>6.23$, compared with $4.28$ for a marginal cut at the same baseline and $3\rho_D=3.51$ at free trade.

\subsection{Finite tariffs}
\label{app:asym-finite}

The thresholds of \cref{prop:unilateral,prop:retaliation} concern marginal tariffs at free trade. In one case the finite-tariff sign is exact. At $N=2$ and $\eta=1$, expenditure shares are fixed at their weights, and $I_A=w_AL_A/[1-\tfrac{\tau}{1+\tau}m]$, so $\TB_A=0$ becomes $e\,m\,w_BL_B=mI_A/(1+\tau)$:
\begin{equation*}
e(\tau)=\frac{w_AL_A}{w_BL_B}\cdot\frac{1}{1+(1-m)\tau},\qquad
\frac{d\log e}{d\tau}\bigg|_{\tau=0}=-(1-m)=-\frac{\rho_D}{M_2}\bigg|_{\eta=1}.
\end{equation*}
Thus $e(\tau)$ is decreasing in $\tau$, and the sign of the linearized response is exact for all tariff levels in this case.

For finite tariffs at $N=3$, starting from the symmetric free-trade benchmark, let $\rho^*(\tau)$ denote the value of $\rho$ at which $\log e_{AC}(\tau)=0$ in the exact model. Writing $\log e_{AC}(\tau)=a(\rho)\tau+\tfrac12b_C\tau^2+O(\tau^3)$, where $a$ is the first-order slope and $b_C$ the second derivative along the tariff path,
\begin{equation*}
\rho^*(\tau)=\rho^*_0+\kappa\,\tau+O(\tau^2),\qquad\kappa=-\frac{b_C}{2a'(\rho^*_0)}.
\end{equation*}
For a unilateral tariff, $a=(\rho-3\rho_D)/(6M_3)$ by \cref{eq:threecountryslopes}, so $\kappa_{\mathrm{uni}}=-3M_3b_C$. For bilateral retaliation, $\tau_{AB}=\tau_{BA}=\tau$ and $a=(\rho-\rho_D)/(2M_3)$ by \cref{eq:retaliation}, so $\kappa_{\mathrm{war}}=-M_3b_C$. Both are evaluated at the symmetric benchmark, where $b_C$ follows from a second-order expansion of the equilibrium conditions.

The unilateral threshold falls with the size of the tariff. On a grid over $\alpha_D\in[1/3,1)$, $\alpha_T\in(0,1]$ and $\eta\in[1,10]$, the exact finite-difference slope $(\rho^*(0.01)-3\rho_D)/0.01$ is negative at every point. At $\tau=1$ the exact threshold is $0.76$ times $3\rho_D$ at the calibration of this appendix and $0.72$ times $3\rho_D$ at US-like parameters; at the 2025 peak rate $\tau=1.45$ it is $2.70$ at US-like parameters, against $3\rho_D=3.96$. A larger tariff shrinks $B$'s share of $A$'s imports, so a further increase adds less average protection while continuing to divert expenditure toward $C$. The retaliation threshold instead rises slightly, with
\begin{equation*}
\kappa_{\mathrm{war}}=\frac{1-\alpha_D}{4}\Big[\alpha_D(\eta-1)^2+3\alpha_D\alpha_T(\eta-1)+2\alpha_T(1-m)\Big]>0\qquad(\eta\geq1),
\end{equation*}
and at $\tau=1$ the exact threshold is $1.07$ times $\rho_D$ ($1.03$ at US-like parameters).

These two subsections illustrate a common principle. The threshold depends on the share structure on which the tariff acts. A common tariff shrinks $A$'s footprint in its partners' trade and raises the threshold, while a concentrated tariff shrinks the target's share of $A$'s imports and lowers it.

\subsection{Asymmetric primitives}
\label{app:asym-primitives}

At a general balanced baseline the coefficients of \cref{eq:linear} are the baseline shares $b_{ij}$, the home shares $h_i$ and the incomes $y_i$. The elasticity $\rho$ enters the share responses only through $(1-\rho)$, so every entry of $J$ and $F$ is affine in $\rho$. Each entry of $\adj(-J)$ is therefore a polynomial of degree at most $N-2$ in $\rho$, and each numerator $[\adj(-J)F]_i$ a polynomial of degree at most $N-1$. Since $\det(-J)>0$ under MML, a bilateral response can change sign only at a root of this numerator. Symmetry reduces the problem to the two coefficients of \cref{prop:decomposition}, yielding linear thresholds. At a general asymmetric baseline the $N=3$ numerator is already quadratic in $\rho$:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{sign}\frac{d\log e_{AC}}{d\tau}=\operatorname{sign}\big(c_2\rho^2+c_1\rho+c_0\big),\qquad c_2=\alpha_T^2\,b_{AB}b_{AC}b_{CA}b_{CB}(1-h_A)(1-h_C)y_Ay_C.
\label{eq:quadratic}
\end{equation}
The coefficients $c_1$ and $c_0$ depend on the full pattern of trade shares and country sizes. Provided MML holds, $c_2>0$ whenever $A$ and $C$ each import from both foreign origins, so at given shares the response is positive beyond the largest positive root, which equals $3\rho_D$ at the symmetric point.

The quadratic holds at given shares, however, and the shares themselves depend on $\rho$ once primitives are held fixed. At an asymmetric baseline relative prices differ across origins, and a higher $\rho$ also reallocates expenditure toward the cheapest origin. A large target, whose currency must depreciate to balance its trade, can then crowd $C$ out of $A$'s imports and prevent the reversal. With $L_B=2.2$ and all other primitives symmetric, $e_{AC}$ reverses only for $5.99<\rho<53.8$, as $A$'s import share from $B$ rises from $0.74$ at $\rho=5$ to $0.997$ at $\rho=100$; with $L_B\geq2.6$ it does not reverse for any $\rho\leq1000$. Away from symmetry there may therefore be no bilateral threshold, one, or several, and for $N\geq4$ the numerator is of higher degree in $\rho$.

The threshold also moves with asymmetric primitives near the symmetric benchmark. \Cref{tab:sens} reports $d\rho^*/dx$ at $\rho=3\rho_D$, where $\rho^*$ solves $d\log e_{AC}/d\tau=0$ after perturbing a single primitive $x$ and recomputing the free-trade baseline. Derivatives are evaluated at $L_i=1$ and $\beta_{AB}=1/2$. Here $\beta_{AB}$ is $A$'s weight on $B$, with $\beta_{AC}=1-\beta_{AB}$, and $\alpha_D^A$ is the home weight in $A$ alone.

\noindent The interpretations are as follows. \emph{$\beta_{AB}$:} a tariff on a larger supplier is a larger average-protection shock, so $B$'s loss rises relative to $C$'s gain. \emph{$L_B$:} a larger target depreciates to balance its trade, which raises $A$'s import share from $B$. \emph{$L_C$:} a larger bystander depreciates and gains import share in $A$, but also absorbs its own disturbance more easily; the two effects nearly offset. \emph{$\alpha_D^A$:} stronger home bias in $A$ raises domestic diversion and makes $C$'s rate respond more to $B$'s loss.

\subsection{The effective rate and the ordering of bilateral responses}
\label{app:asym-neer}

At the symmetric benchmark, relative treatment has no effect on the equally weighted average of $A$'s bilateral responses, and this average coincides with the trade-weighted NEER. A unilateral tariff therefore always appreciates $A$'s effective exchange rate. Relative treatment drops out of the effective rate only when the trade weights and the adjustment structure are symmetric. With asymmetric trade weights, $d\nu^E_A=w_{AB}\,d\nu_B+w_{AC}\,d\nu_C$, and a bystander depreciation $d\nu_C>0$ enters with weight $w_{AC}$. The trade-weighted NEER can then depreciate when the bystander reversal occurs and the bystander carries most of $A$'s trade weight. \Cref{tab:neer} illustrates this at US-like parameters. The reversal starts below $3\rho_D$ because a small target with a small import weight lowers the threshold, as in \cref{tab:sens}. The exact model at $\tau=0.01$ confirms the signs.

The ordering $d\nu_B<d\nu_C$, which holds at the symmetric benchmark because $\lambda_R<0$, can also fail. This requires $\rho$ near one and a high $\eta$, and $\rho<\eta$ in every case we examined. For example, with $L=(5.12,4.26,2.28)$ for $(A,B,C)$, $\beta_{AB}=0.86$, $\beta_{BA}=0.65$, $\beta_{CA}=0.19$, $\alpha_D=0.89$, $\alpha_T=0.6$, $\eta=3.74$ and $\rho=1.20$, $d\nu_B-d\nu_C=0.08\,d\tau$ (MML holds; the exact model at $\tau=0.01$ gives $0.079$).
