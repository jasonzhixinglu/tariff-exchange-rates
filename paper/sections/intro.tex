\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

What happens to exchange rates when a country imposes a tariff? In the standard two-country model, a tariff shifts expenditure away from imports and improves the trade balance at the prevailing exchange rate. Under the Marshall--Lerner condition, external balance is restored through an appreciation of the tariffing country's currency \citep{dornbusch1974,krugman1982}. Because there is only one trading partner, the bilateral exchange rate is also the effective exchange rate. In the long run, in real terms, this same relative price also governs the terms of trade. Thus, in the simplest bilateral setting, the bilateral exchange rate, the effective exchange rate, and the long-run terms of trade all necessarily coincide.

These concepts separate once trade is multilateral. A tariff on one partner changes not only the relative price of foreign and domestic goods, but also relative prices across foreign suppliers. It can therefore affect the exchange rate against the target, the rates against untariffed bystanders, and the effective exchange rate differently. In any differentiable multi-country trade environment, we show that the first-order response can be represented as the adjustment of exchange rates to a vector of tariff-induced trade-balance disturbances. We define a multilateral Marshall--Lerner condition, implied by gross substitutability of trade balances in currency values, that ensures well-behaved adjustment. Crucially, we show that even under this condition, the sign of an individual bilateral exchange-rate response is not determined. A tariff therefore need not appreciate the tariffing country's currency against every partner.

We first answer this question in a general trade environment under symmetry among trading partners. We show that any vector of multilateral tariff changes decomposes into average protection and relative treatment. Average protection uniquely determines the change in the nominal effective exchange rate, while relative treatment determines the relative changes in bilateral exchange rates. Under a unilateral tariff, these two components reinforce each other against the targeted partner but work in opposite directions against untariffed bystanders. The response against bystanders is therefore ambiguous and depends on the structure of the trade environment.

To characterize these counteracting effects, we develop a nested CES Armington model that distinguishes substitution between domestic and foreign goods from substitution across foreign suppliers. Substitution toward domestic goods supports the conventional appreciation, while substitution across foreign suppliers shifts expenditure toward untariffed countries and strengthens their currencies relative to the tariffing country. Allowing these two margins to differ, we show that the tariffing country can depreciate against an untariffed bystander when cross-foreign substitution is sufficiently strong relative to home-foreign substitution, but, under weak home bias, this outcome cannot arise with a single elasticity across domestic and foreign varieties. Empirical estimates distinguishing cross-foreign from home-foreign substitution support treating these margins separately \citep{broda2006,feenstra2018,li2022}.

The same mechanisms extend naturally from unilateral tariffs to retaliation and multilateral trade wars. When the target retaliates with an equal tariff, the average-protection effects on the bilateral exchange rate cancel, while the relative-treatment effects reinforce each other against third countries. Retaliation therefore strengthens the relative-treatment force and lowers the threshold for depreciation against bystanders from $\rho>N\rho_D$ under a unilateral tariff to $\rho>\rho_D$, where $\rho$ is the elasticity of substitution across foreign suppliers and $\rho_D$ is a domestic diversion margin. In a multilateral trade war, in which every partner retaliates against the tariffing country, the same condition leads it to depreciate against every partner.

Our trade framework is deliberately stylized and is not intended to provide a quantitative account of the 2025 tariff episode, but it nonetheless offers a new perspective on the exchange-rate movements during this period. Our application to 2025 is neither a calibration nor a causal test: it compares the sign patterns the model associates with each phase's tariff configuration with the observed exchange-rate movements. The dollar's depreciation following the tariff announcements was initially interpreted in part as evidence of higher risk premia, declining convenience yields, and concerns about the dollar's global role \citep{itskhoki2025,kalemliozcan2026}. Applying our framework to the broad features of the episode, we show that dollar depreciation need not originate outside the trade channel, particularly when retaliation is expected. Consistent with this possibility, \citet{ostry2025} document that the dollar tends to appreciate following unilateral US tariffs, depreciate when trading partners retaliate, and remain comparatively stable against the renminbi during US--China tit-for-tat episodes. They hypothesize that dominant-currency pricing may help explain this pattern. Our results show that the same pattern can arise from canonical trade substitution and diversion effects once trade and exchange-rate adjustment is considered in a multilateral setting.

In a general asymmetric setting, where economies differ in size, trade shares, and productivities, exchange-rate responses no longer admit simple analytical expressions that determine when average protection or relative treatment dominates. Uniform protection nevertheless retains the conventional local aggregate result: starting from balanced free trade, a small uniform tariff appreciates the tariffing country's currency weakly against every partner under the multilateral Marshall--Lerner condition, improves its aggregate terms of trade, and raises welfare to first order. Discriminatory protection can instead overturn the aggregate result. In our numerical comparative statics, reversals arise most naturally when the target is small or lightly weighted, the bystanders account for a large share of trade, and cross-origin substitution is sufficiently strong.

Our paper builds on the classical analysis of tariffs under flexible exchange rates, which identifies the conditions under which tariffs lead to exchange-rate appreciation \citep{lerner1936,metzler1949,mundell1961,dornbusch1974,eichengreen1981,krugman1982}. A large open-economy macroeconomics literature extends this analysis to environments with nominal rigidities, monetary policy, currency invoicing, and alternative uses of tariff revenue \citep{linde2019,barattieri2021,erceg2023,bergin2023,werning2025}. We instead revisit the flexible-price benchmark in a multilateral setting, where tariffs affect not only the importing country's aggregate exchange rate but also relative exchange rates across trading partners. Our bystander results are related to the literature on third-country effects of preferential trade policy, particularly the roles of trade diversion and competition among exporters \citep{viner1950,mundell1964,bond1996,bagwell1999,bagwell2002}. We show how these forces affect bilateral and effective exchange rates when the importing country also produces a competing good. Finally, our multilateral Marshall--Lerner condition draws on the classical literature on stability in multiple markets \citep{hicks1939,mosak1944,metzler1945,arrow1958}, applying these conditions to the exchange-rate response to tariffs in a multilateral setting.

Our results also relate to the empirical literature on the exchange-rate effects of tariffs, including the 2025 episode \citep{itskhoki2025,kalemliozcan2026,ostry2025} and earlier episodes \citep{furceri2019,matveev2021,jeanne2024}. Our analysis also offers a complementary perspective on the empirical literature estimating tariff pass-through into import prices \citep{amiti2019,fajgelbaum2020,cavallo2021}. This literature typically uses dollar-denominated import prices as a proxy for tariff incidence. In a multilateral setting, however, exchange-rate movements provide an additional margin of incidence through changes in relative prices. An exporter whose dollar price is unchanged may still experience a decline in the home-currency value of its receipts when the dollar depreciates. Assessing tariff incidence from import prices alone can therefore overlook adjustment through exchange rates, a margin that may have been particularly relevant during the broad dollar depreciation in 2025.

This paper is organized as follows. \Cref{sec:framework} develops the general multilateral adjustment framework and decomposes tariff changes into average protection and relative treatment. \Cref{sec:nestedces} derives the results for unilateral tariffs in a nested-CES model. \Cref{sec:tradewars} analyzes retaliation, connects the results to preferential trade, and applies the framework to the 2025 tariff episode. \Cref{sec:asymmetry} examines asymmetry. \Cref{sec:conclusion} concludes. Derivations and proofs are in the appendix, and secondary results are in an online supplement.
