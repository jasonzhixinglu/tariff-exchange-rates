\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

What happens to exchange rates when a country imposes a tariff? In the standard two-country model, a tariff shifts expenditure away from imports and improves the trade balance at the prevailing exchange rate. Under the Marshall--Lerner condition, external balance is restored through an appreciation of the tariffing country's currency \citep{dornbusch1974,krugman1982}. Because there is only one trading partner, the bilateral exchange rate is also the effective exchange rate. In the long run, in real terms, this same relative price also governs the terms of trade. Thus, in the simplest bilateral setting, the bilateral exchange rate, the effective exchange rate, and the long-run terms of trade all necessarily coincide.

These concepts separate once trade is multilateral. A tariff on one partner changes not only the relative price of foreign and domestic goods, but also relative prices across foreign suppliers. It can therefore affect the exchange rate against the target, the rates against untariffed bystanders, and the effective exchange rate differently. In any differentiable multi-country trade environment, we show that the first-order response can be represented as the adjustment of exchange rates to a vector of tariff-induced trade-balance disturbances. We define a multilateral Marshall--Lerner condition, implied by gross substitutability of trade balances in currency values, that ensures well-behaved adjustment. Crucially, we show that even under this condition, the sign of an individual bilateral exchange-rate response is not determined. A tariff therefore need not appreciate the tariffing country's currency against the targeted partner.

We first answer this question in a general trade environment under symmetry among trading partners. We show that any vector of multilateral tariff changes decomposes into average protection and relative treatment. Average protection uniquely determines the change in the nominal effective exchange rate, while relative treatment determines the relative changes in bilateral exchange rates. Under a unilateral tariff, these two components reinforce each other against the targeted partner but work in opposite directions against untariffed bystanders. The response against bystanders is therefore ambiguous and depends on the structure of the trade environment.

The bystander response depends on substitution toward domestic goods relative to substitution across foreign suppliers. Expenditure switching toward domestic goods supports the conventional appreciation. Diversion toward untariffed suppliers instead strengthens their currencies relative to the tariffing country. A countervailing force operates through the target. As the target loses access to the tariffing country's market, its currency depreciates, making its goods cheaper for third countries and placing downward pressure on bystander currencies. We establish that the tariffing country depreciates against a bystander only when substitution across foreign suppliers is sufficiently strong relative to domestic diversion. The reversal requires foreign suppliers to be closer substitutes for one another than foreign goods are for domestic goods and cannot arise, under standard home-bias conditions, in a specification with a single elasticity across all tradable varieties. Empirical estimates that distinguish cross-foreign from home-foreign substitution support treating these margins separately \citep{broda2006,feenstra2018,li2022}.

At the symmetric free-trade benchmark, we prove that a unilateral tariff appreciates the tariffing country's currency against the target and appreciates its nominal effective exchange rate under the multilateral Marshall--Lerner condition. Yet the tariff can depreciate the currency against every untariffed bystander when cross-origin substitution is sufficiently strong. We also establish an exact first-order reduction to a three-region economy consisting of the tariffing country, the target, and a composite bystander. The reduction shows that the number of countries matters not simply because the bystander bloc is larger, but because more of the bloc's trade is internal and highly substitutable. This result qualifies the competing-exporter logic in the preferential-trade literature, under which protection against one exporter improves the terms of trade of an excluded exporter \citep{viner1950,mundell1964}. When the importing country also produces a competing good, that third-country effect becomes conditional on foreign substitution being sufficiently strong. Despite a possible depreciation against bystanders, we prove that a unilateral tariff at the symmetric benchmark improves the tariffing country's aggregate terms of trade and raises welfare to first order.

Retaliation changes these conclusions. Under symmetry, we prove that equal bilateral tariffs leave the exchange rate between the two belligerents unchanged to first order, while adjustment occurs against third countries. The degree of foreign substitution required for depreciation is then substantially lower than under a unilateral tariff. When substitution across foreign suppliers exceeds the domestic-diversion margin, both belligerents depreciate against bystanders and in effective terms. In a multilateral trade war, the same condition leads the tariffing country to depreciate against every partner. The real trade channel can therefore generate effective appreciation under unilateral protection, bilateral stability under retaliation, and broad depreciation during a trade war without nominal or financial frictions.

The 2025 tariff episode provides the immediate motivation for revisiting the conventional benchmark. The dollar weakened against several major currencies following the tariff announcements while moving comparatively little against the renminbi. Several early analyses took the conventional appreciation result as their starting point and interpreted dollar weakness primarily through risk premia, convenience yields, valuation effects, or tariff uncertainty \citep{itskhoki2025,kalemliozcan2026}. These mechanisms may have been important and are complementary to our analysis. Our point is narrower: the two-country result is not sufficient to conclude that a multilateral depreciation must originate outside the trade channel.

The empirical evidence also highlights the role of policy configuration. \citet{ostry2025} document that the dollar tends to appreciate following unilateral US tariffs, depreciate when partners retaliate, and remain broadly stable against the renminbi during US--China tit-for-tat episodes. They interpret this pattern through dominant-currency pricing. We establish the same qualitative configuration dependence through real trade diversion at the symmetric benchmark. A unilateral tariff appreciates the effective exchange rate, while bilateral retaliation can leave the belligerents' exchange rate unchanged and depreciate both currencies against bystanders and in effective terms. The two mechanisms can coexist.

We use the model to provide a stylized interpretation of the 2025 episode, not a quantitative explanation or empirical test. The early phase combined discriminatory US tariffs on China with tariffs and retaliation in North America. The relative stability of the dollar against China and its weakness against important bystanders are consistent with the trade channel when substitution across foreign suppliers is strong, although the mixed policy configuration prevents a clean interpretation. The subsequent US--China escalation more closely approximated bilateral retaliation, for which our symmetric results predict a stable bilateral rate together with depreciation against bystanders and in effective terms. Later, US tariffs became more nearly uniform across partners. Our general uniform-tariff result implies that the increasingly uniform component should have supported effective appreciation, while remaining tariff differentials and retaliation worked in the opposite direction. The absence of clear appreciation in that phase leaves a role for forces outside the model. The episode must therefore be assessed phase by phase rather than against a single two-country prediction.

Several aggregate relationships continue to overlap under symmetry. Although bilateral rates can move differently from the effective rate, the trade weights used in the nominal effective exchange rate coincide at the symmetric benchmark with the import weights relevant for the aggregate terms of trade. Effective appreciation, a terms-of-trade improvement, and the first-order welfare gain therefore remain aligned. The exact composite-bystander representation also continues to apply. Local comparative statics and numerical exercises indicate that the main symmetric sign results are robust to moderate asymmetry, although the relevant thresholds vary with country size, trade weights, and home bias.

Strong asymmetry can overturn these aggregate conclusions. Away from symmetry, bilateral rates, the trade-weighted effective exchange rate, and the import-weighted aggregate terms of trade use different weights and need not move together. The bystander bloc can no longer generally be represented as a single country, and the relationship between foreign substitution and exchange-rate adjustment need not be monotonic. We show that when the target is small or carries little weight in the tariffing country's imports, and foreign suppliers are sufficiently close substitutes, depreciations against more heavily weighted bystanders can outweigh the appreciation against the target. A unilateral tariff can then worsen the aggregate terms of trade and reduce welfare. Under stronger asymmetry, when bystanders also account for most of the tariffing country's trade, we show that even the nominal effective appreciation obtained under symmetry can reverse.

These reversals apply to discriminatory tariffs, not to uniform protection. Under general conditions allowing country sizes, trade shares, home bias, and preference parameters to differ, we prove that a small uniform tariff imposed from a balanced free-trade baseline appreciates the tariffing country's currency weakly against every partner under the multilateral Marshall--Lerner condition. It therefore improves the aggregate terms of trade and raises welfare to first order. This result recovers the classical terms-of-trade argument for uniform protection while showing why its partner-specific counterpart can fail. Uniform protection eliminates relative treatment across suppliers and switches off the cross-origin diversion mechanism that drives the reversals.

\subsection{Related literature}
\label{sec:literature}

A rapidly growing literature studies the exchange-rate effects of the 2025 tariffs through financial channels, retaliation and rich quantitative models; what it lacks, and what we provide, is a characterization of what the real trade channel \emph{itself} implies for bilateral and effective exchange rates once trade is multilateral.

\paragraph{Tariffs and exchange rates.} The classical two-country analysis of tariffs under flexible exchange rates \citep{lerner1936,metzler1949,mundell1961,dornbusch1974,eichengreen1981,krugman1982} delivers the appreciation result; \citet{dornbusch1974} is particularly close to us in giving non-traded goods a role in the incidence of a tariff, which enters our domestic diversion margin through the rebate. \citet{costinot2019} give the modern statement of Lerner symmetry; since our results concern the real allocation, they are unaffected by Lerner-type reformulations of the policy. A large open-macroeconomy literature studies tariffs in the short run with nominal rigidities, where monetary policy, invoicing and the fiscal use of revenue shape the response \citep{linde2019,barattieri2021,erceg2023,jeanne2021,bergin2023,auray2025,bianchi2025,werning2025}. We complement that work with the long-run real benchmark in a multilateral setting, the position toward which those short-run dynamics converge once prices have adjusted.

\paragraph{The 2025 dollar.} The financial and uncertainty explanations of the 2025 dollar \citep{kalemliozcan2026,acharya2026,itskhoki2025,cardani2025,jiang2025} are complements to our analysis, not competitors; \citet{kalemliozcan2026}, for instance, work in a two-country model, where tariff levels appreciate the currency and tariff volatility depreciates it. \citet{itskhoki2025} are the closest theory paper on the dollar: in their static benchmark a tariff appreciates the currency, and with dollar-denominated US liabilities that appreciation carries a valuation cost that sharply lowers the optimal macro tariff; a tariff war can then cost the United States its exorbitant privilege. Their mechanism is valuation and convenience yield; ours is real trade diversion. The paper closest to ours empirically is \citet{ostry2025}. Building a database of tariff announcements and retaliations for 2018--20 and 2025, they show that the dollar appreciates on unilateral US tariffs and depreciates when partners retaliate, while in US--China tit-for-tat exchanges it is roughly flat bilaterally. They interpret the pattern through dominant-currency pricing \citep{gopinath2020}. Our bilateral-retaliation result predicts exactly the same pattern, with $d\nu_B=0$ against the retaliating partner and depreciation against bystanders whenever $\rho>\rho_D$, without any nominal friction. The two explanations can coexist. Ours shows that the configuration-dependence they document is what the real trade channel requires in the long run, so it need not be read as evidence of a nominal or financial friction, and it identifies the substitution conditions under which that reading is warranted.

\paragraph{Third-country effects.} Because the exchange rate \emph{is} the terms of trade in our model class, the preferential-trade-agreement literature on third-country terms-of-trade effects applies directly \citep{viner1950,mundell1964,kemp1976,krugman1991,bond1996,bagwell1999,bagwell2002,yi2000,chang2002,ornelas2005}. Our contribution to it is narrow: the competing-exporter result, in which the excluded exporter's terms of trade always improve, becomes conditional ($\rho>N\rho_D$) once the importer also produces a competing good. Quantitative and empirical work on the third-country effects of trade wars \citep{darracqparies2026,ignatenko2025,fajgelbaum2024,freund2024,alfaro2023,fajgelbaum2026} quantifies these effects in rich environments; \citet{darracqparies2026} conclude that tariff incidence is intrinsically multilateral and cannot be inferred from two-country analysis. These papers quantify; we characterize signs. Our uniform-tariff result also connects to optimal-tariff theory: a country with market power gains from a small tariff \citep{johnson1953,broda2008,costinot2015}, and we recover this for a uniform tariff at any asymmetric baseline, whereas the partner-by-partner version can fail.

\paragraph{Elasticities.} Our thresholds turn on the gap between the home--import (macro) and cross-origin (micro) elasticities. The evidence that cross-foreign elasticities exceed home--foreign ones \citep{broda2006,feenstra2018,li2022}, and that long-run trade elasticities are larger than short-run ones \citep{boehm2023}, is exactly the distinction our results require.

\paragraph{Multi-market stability.} The MML condition descends from the Hicks--Metzler--Mosak conditions for the stability of multiple markets and from the gross-substitutes stability results \citep{hicks1939,mosak1944,metzler1945,arrow1958,arrow1959}. We apply that lineage to the tariff--exchange-rate mapping rather than inventing the condition; the IMF's multilateral exchange rate model \citep{artus1973} is an early institutional antecedent of multilateral exchange-rate modelling of this kind.

\paragraph{Roadmap.} This paper is organized as follows. \Cref{sec:framework} develops the general disturbance-adjustment framework and the multilateral Marshall--Lerner condition, and derives the symmetric decomposition into average protection and relative treatment. \Cref{sec:nestedces} gives the decomposition economic content in a nested-CES model, establishes the unilateral-tariff results, and derives the exact three-region representation. \Cref{sec:tradewars} studies bilateral retaliation, multilateral trade wars, and preferential tariffs. \Cref{sec:asymmetry} introduces asymmetric trade relationships, distinguishes the effective exchange rate from the welfare-relevant aggregate terms of trade, establishes the general uniform-tariff result, and applies the framework to the 2025 episode. \Cref{sec:conclusion} concludes. The appendices contain the derivations, proofs, and robustness results.
